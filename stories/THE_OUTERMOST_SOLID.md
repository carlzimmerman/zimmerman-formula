# The Outermost Solid

*a short story*

---

The trouble with the cube was that it kept wanting to be a room.

Johannes had been at it since Candlemas, and the wire would not hold its corners. He had drawn
the thing ten thousand times — the cube between Saturn and Jupiter, the outermost of the five
solids, the first partition God had set into the emptiness — and on paper it was obedient.
In brass wire it sagged. It slouched. It became, no matter how carefully he soldered, a sort of
lopsided birdcage.

He had told no one what he was building. He had told Susanna it was an orrery.

The sphere had come first, and the sphere had come by accident, which was how most true things
arrived. He had been trying to freeze a lens. Some fool in Ulm had written him about glass that
grew colder the more light you passed through it, and Johannes, who could not leave a wrong idea
alone, had set out to prove the man an ass. He had proved him an ass. But in the proving, on the
ninth of February, at the hour when the fire had gone down and his fingers had gone numb, the
thing in the crucible had stopped behaving like glass.

It went clear. Then it went *more* clear — clear the way water is clear when you have forgotten
the glass is there — and then it began, very faintly, to shine. Not warm. That was the part he
wrote down four times in four different hands, as though one of them might make it plausible.
*Non calidus.* Cold light. He put his palm against it and felt winter.

He spent eleven days learning that it would not break, would not scratch, and would hold a shape
if you asked it politely. He spent four more learning that it wanted a shape. It had been waiting
for one.

So: the cube. Because of course the cube. Because if the heavens were built of nested solids then
the outermost was the cube, and if you were going to put a lattice inside a sphere you did not
choose the lattice at random like a man buying turnips.

On the twenty-third he got the corners to hold. He soldered the last joint at three in the
morning, and when he lowered the brass frame into the glowing shell the light did not brighten —
it *organised*. It ran along the wire like water finding a channel. It sat in the eight corners
and hummed.

Johannes sat back on his heels on the cold floor of a rented house in Linz and understood, with
the particular loneliness of a man who is right, that he had made a vehicle.

---

He should have tested it with a mouse.

He knew this even as he was climbing in — the shell parted for him like a held breath and closed
again without a seam — and he knew it as he took hold of the lattice with both hands, the wire
cool and faintly singing under his palms. He had one apple and a heel of bread in his coat
pocket, because he had dressed that morning expecting a day of failure and had packed for it.
He was fifty-one years old. His eyes were bad. His mother had been tried as a witch two years
before and he had spent fourteen months writing her out of the fire, and he was so tired, and the
cube was humming, and he thought: *just to the roofline. Just to see.*

The house fell away like a dropped coin.

He did not scream. He has been quite clear about this, in the pages, several times. He did not
scream — he made a sound, and the sound was not fear, and there is no Latin for it.

Linz became a smudge with a river through it. Then the river became a thread, and the thread
became a crack in a great curved plate of brown and green, and then the plate curved further and
kept curving and would not stop, and he saw the edge of it go white and blue at the same time,
and he understood the second thing: *it is a ball. Of course it is a ball. I have said so in print
for twenty years and I have never once believed it in my stomach.*

The Earth hung under him in the cold light of his own sphere. There were clouds on it, and the
clouds had shadows, and the shadows were on the sea. He could see weather happening. He could see
it *from outside*, the way a man sees the top of a table.

He hung onto the cube and wept, which cost him his spectacles, and then he laughed, because he
could see perfectly well without them out here, and he had spent forty years and a great deal of
money on the wrong problem.

---

After that he went to look at everything.

He would not, later, be able to put the order of it right. There was the rusted one, with the
dust storms you could watch turn like a slow wheel, and the two little moons that went the wrong
way about. There was the striped giant with the red wound in its side that had been raging, he
worked out, since before there were people to name it, and the four bright children he had argued
about with the Florentine. He went close enough to the ringed one to see that the rings were not
rings — that they were *rubble*, uncountable, a shattered thing still politely orbiting the shape
of what it had been — and he thought about the cube between Saturn and Jupiter, his cube, the
outermost solid, and how a man could be beautifully and completely wrong and still get himself
into the sky by it.

He went out past the cold ones. He went out past the last of them, the little frozen pebble that
nobody in his century had any business knowing about, and he stopped and looked back at the sun
and found that it had become a star.

Then he turned the cube along its long diagonal, the way you tilt a hat, and went to see the
others.

There were so many. That is the part he could never get into words that satisfied him, and he
tried, in the pages, for the rest of his life. There were worlds with two suns and worlds with
none. There were worlds of nothing but ocean and one of nothing but a single continuous storm and
one — he watched it for what he thought was an afternoon — where something was building
something, in patterns, on the night side. He did not go down. He was a Lutheran and a
mathematician and he understood a boundary when he saw one.

He ate the apple somewhere past the third arm of it. He kept the core, out of a habit he could not
explain, and he still had it in his pocket when he came home.

---

He knew before he landed. That was the cruelty of it: he was the man who had found the laws, and
so of course he did the arithmetic on the way down and did not like the answer.

He had gone fast. He had gone so fast that the going had cost him nothing — a season, two at the
most, a summer's worth of hunger — and the sums said that a summer for a man on the lattice was
not a summer for the man's house. The faster the cheaper, for him. The faster the dearer, for
everyone he had ever spoken to.

He came down through cloud over a country he did not recognise, over roads too straight and too
grey and full of small bright beetles hurrying, and he brought the sphere down as gently as he
knew how in the middle of the greenest, flattest, most impossibly *even* patch of grass he had
ever seen. It was cut. Not grazed — *cut*, all to one height, in stripes, like a bolt of cloth.
No animal had done that. He knelt and put his hand on it and it was warm and soft and springy and
it had been loved by someone with a great deal of time.

Zoysia, though he did not know the word. It would not have helped.

The house behind it was low and wide and had enormous windows and no shutters, and there was a
sort of carriage in front of it with no horse and no shafts and no visible business being there.
The light in the windows was steady. Not flame-steady. *Steady.*

He stood up. He brushed the grass from his knees, which was a thing he had done ten thousand
times in his life and which now seemed to him an extraordinary piece of luck. He had a bread heel
and an apple core in his pocket and no money of any century and a brass cube in a sphere of cold
light on a stranger's lawn.

He walked up the path and knocked on the door.

It opened almost at once, which surprised him. A man stood in it — clean-shaven, in soft strange
clothes, holding a cup — and he looked at Johannes for a moment: at the ruff, at the worn coat,
at the bad eyes, at the glowing thing on his grass.

"Good evening," Johannes said, in the best Latin he had, and then, in German, and then in a
halting careful something he had picked up from the shapes of the roads: "Hello. My name is
Johannes Kepler."

The man did not move. He set the cup down on a little table beside the door, without looking at
it, without hurry.

"Yes," he said. "We have been expecting you."

---

*Kepler published* Somnium *— a dream of a voyage to the Moon — in 1634, four years after his
death. He is generally credited with having written the first work of science fiction. He never
mentioned the cube.*
