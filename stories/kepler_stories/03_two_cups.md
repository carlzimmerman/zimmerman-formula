# Two Cups

*Chapter three*

---

The house was all on one floor.

That was the first thing, and he noticed it the way a mathematician notices a missing term. In Linz you built upward, because land was dear and a shared wall was a wall you did not have to pay for. This house simply went *sideways*. It ran long and low under a broad roof, as if the man who built it had owned more land than he could ever want and had seen no reason to climb. Johannes had crossed a deep front porch to reach the door. On it, two rocking chairs sat side by side, waiting for no one, and above them a fan with blades of wood turned slowly by itself. Nobody was pulling a cord.

Inside, the floor was laid in wide planks of pale wood, and on the planks lay Turkish carpets.

He stopped dead.

He knew Turkish carpets. In Prague a man of rank who owned one laid it over his table, never under his feet, and had his portrait painted sitting beside it so the neighbours would know. These people had three of them in this room alone, in madder red and saffron and indigo and a green like river weed, and they were simply lying on the floor, under the furniture, being *walked on*. Johannes stepped around the nearest one, then felt foolish, and then stepped around it anyway.

Along one wall stood a low table, and on it a row of vases glazed the exact blue-green of turquoise. A Prague jeweller would have charged him a month's salary for a thumbnail of that colour. Someone had filled them, quite carelessly, with branches as thick as his wrist, bearing great white flowers and leaves like polished leather.

The kitchen was open to the rest of the room, which no kitchen he had ever stood in had dared to be. In its middle stood a long block topped with a single slab of pale stone, grey veins running through it like weather on a map. Its edge had been cut and polished so cleanly that when he ran his thumb along it he could not find where the polish ended and the stone began. Who quarried a stone that size and carried it indoors? Who cut it *straight*?

Behind it, the wall was laid with small white tiles, hundreds of them. Each was set at an angle to its neighbour, so the whole wall ran in a zigzag like the spine of a fish. Not one was out of true. Johannes had once spent three weeks trying to make a brass cube hold its corners. He looked at the tiles and felt, briefly and specifically, outclassed.

The chairs were worse. They were not chairs so much as low, broad, padded banks the colour of oatmeal, heaped with cushions in blue and white that no Christian could possibly need. He was quite sure that if he sat in one he would not rise again before Easter.

And the light came from nowhere: from the ceiling, from little recesses, from the walls themselves. It was steady and white, and nothing burned to make it.

It was, he would learn much later, exactly the kind of house that people in that part of the world photographed for magazines, so that other people could want it.

The man had stood quietly by the door, watching him look. He did not seem to mind.

"Come this way," he said.

---

The hall was long, as everything in this house was long, and down its middle ran another carpet, narrow as a church aisle. Johannes walked on it with a guilty step, the way a man walks across a grave, in his worn shoes, very aware of his ruff.

On his left the hall had a window, tall and narrow. For perhaps the space of two steps he saw the lawn through it.

Four men in dark suits, as alike as crows, were moving his sphere across the grass toward a long white wagon with its rear doors open. The sphere had gone dark. It no longer glowed. It looked like what it was: a bubble of strange glass with a bent brass frame inside, caved in along one side where he had come down harder than he had admitted to himself. They were wheeling it on some low, flat cart, slowly, the way pallbearers walk.

He almost stopped. He almost said something.

Then the window was behind him, and the man ahead of him had not turned around. Johannes found he was too tired for any feeling but a kind of numb gratitude, so he did what he had always done with a thing he could not yet explain. He filed it under *later*, and he kept walking.

---

The bedroom was plain and clean and smelled faintly of nothing at all. A quilt of faded blue squares lay folded at the foot of the bed, and on the bed, folded with the care of someone who had practised, lay a set of men's clothes: trousers of heavy blue cloth, a shirt with small hard buttons, a soft grey jersey, stockings, linen. A pair of shoes waited on the floor beside them, laces tucked in.

Beyond the bedroom was a little room of glass and stone. The man stepped inside it and laid his hand on a silver lever set into the wall.

"Pull this back for the water to flow," he said. "Move it to the left for colder, and to the right for hotter."

He said it in German, and in perfect German. Better, if Johannes was honest, than his own, which still had Württemberg in its vowels after thirty years of trying to sound like a man from somewhere more important. It was nobody's German he had ever heard. It was German the way the tiles were tiles: every piece exactly where it ought to be.

Then the man nodded once, as if to a colleague, and left him.

Johannes pulled the lever back.

Water fell from the ceiling like rain, and the rain was *hot*. There was no fire. No kettle, no servant, no boy staggering up the stairs with two pails. Hot rain, indoors, on demand, from a silver lever turned to the right. He stood under it in his shirt for a full minute before it occurred to him to take the shirt off, and then he laughed, and the laughing turned into something else, and the water took that away too.

He stood there until his fingers wrinkled. He thought of his mother, who had spent fourteen months in chains in a cold tower and would have wept to stand where he stood. He thought of Susanna, and that there was no use thinking of Susanna. Then he made himself stop thinking at all, because he had been thinking without pause since Candlemas, and look where it had got him.

The blue trousers were stiff, and the buttons took some study, but he was a man who had worked out the orbit of Mars. He mastered the buttons.

He took the apple core and the heel of bread from his old coat and set them side by side on the little table by the bed, like a man emptying his pockets in a strange inn. Then he lay down on top of the covers, only for a moment. Only to see.

He was asleep before he finished the thought.

---

He woke to light.

Not candlelight, not dawn through a shutter, but a wide pale wash of morning through glass. For a long moment he did not know where he was, or when, and the second question frightened him far more than the first. He felt as if he had slept a hundred years. That, he would later reflect, was not so very far off.

Then the smell reached him.

It was dark and roasted and bitter, like bread left too long in the oven and somehow forgiven for it. He had never smelled it in his life. It pulled him out of the bed and through the door and down the hall like a fish on a line.

The man sat at the kitchen table, a long table of old scarred wood set by the big front window, in a wash of morning light. In front of him were two cups, and steam rose from both.

"Good morning," said the man.

"Guten Morgen," said Kepler.

He sat. The man pushed one cup toward him. Johannes looked down into it: black as ink, steaming, and smelling of the roasted bitterness that had dragged him out of bed.

"What is this?"

"Coffee," said the man. "You'll like it."

Johannes took it in both hands, because it was warm and his hands were still the hands of a man who had lived his whole life in unheated rooms. He drank.

It tasted like burnt bread and good news. He drank again. The man, he noticed, was trying not to smile.

Through the big window he could see the front lawn, the green striped lawn where he had come down out of the sky. He remembered it exactly: the gouge torn in the turf, the brown earth thrown up in a wave, the scorched ring where the sphere had settled and hissed. He had knelt beside it. He had touched it.

It was gone.

In its place someone had laid fresh grass in strips, edge to edge, rolled out like bolts of cloth. The seams were so fine that he found them only by the way the morning light lay slightly differently across them. If he had not known where to look, he would never have seen them. Someone had worked through the night while he slept, very carefully, so that no one walking past would ever know a man had fallen out of 1622 onto this particular lawn.

He set the cup down.

"What is your name?" he said.

"Everett," said the man.

He let Johannes look at him a moment longer, then folded his hands on the table.

"You know, we've been waiting for you for a long time," Everett said. "In fact, the year is 2026."

Kepler picked up his coffee and drank, because there was nothing else to do with his hands. Behind his eyes the arithmetic ran by itself, the way it always had. He did not want the answer. Four hundred and four years.

"It is a long time," he said.

"It is," said Everett. "And you are living in a new world. Quite literally."

Johannes looked at the stone counter, the tiles, the carpets on the floor, and the silver lever he could still feel in his palm.

"Then tell me one thing," he said. "One thing, and I will believe the rest. Have they solved it? The transmutation. Lead into gold."

He had not meant to ask. He had spent half his life sneering at alchemists. He had watched them come and go at Rudolf's court in Prague: men with singed eyebrows and confident hands, who stayed exactly as long as the Emperor's patience and the Emperor's silver. But he had also spent half his life waiting for the Imperial treasury to pay him, and it still owed him more florins than he could count without a pencil.

"Yes," said Everett.

Kepler's eyes lit up like a man who has just seen the ellipse.

"We can make gold," Everett said. "Truly make it, atom by atom. You take a heavier metal and knock a few pieces off it, inside a machine about the size of a city."

"Then you are all rich."

"That's the problem." Everett turned his cup a quarter turn on the wood. "It costs far more to make a single grain of gold that way than to dig a whole bar of it out of the ground. The alchemists were right that it could be done. They were only wrong that it would ever be worth doing."

Kepler sat back. The light went out of his eyes as quickly as it had come, and for a moment he looked exactly like a man who has been told his back pay will arrive next quarter.

"The Emperor would have built the machine anyway," he said.

"I don't doubt it," said Everett.

He leaned forward.

"When you disappeared, all those years ago, nobody believed it. People said it was a myth. That it couldn't have happened, that you had wandered off, or died somewhere and been forgotten. But a small group of us, independent scientists like myself, have been working on this problem for a very, very long time. And we were finally able to calculate your trajectory." He paused. "We knew you would be here. On this day."

Johannes turned the cup slowly on the old wood.

He had spent his life calculating the paths of things that could not hear him. Mars. The Moon. The comet of 1607. It had never once occurred to him that someone, someday, might sit down with pen and patience and calculate *his*. He found that he did not know how he felt about it. He found that he rather liked it.

"Listen, Johannes," said Everett. "We live in a world now where truth is rare. Do you understand?"

Johannes thought of a brass grate in Prague that had agreed with every idea he ever brought it, until his ideas disappeared into its agreement like stones into a well. He thought of Fabricius smiling at him across a supper table. He thought of a courtroom in Leonberg, and an old woman shown the instruments of torture so that she would say a thing that was not true. And of how long, and how alone, he had had to argue before anyone would simply *look*.

"Yes," he said. "I do."

"Then will you help me?"

"What do you need help with?"

Everett looked at him across the two cups, and for the first time something in his steady face gave way, just slightly. It was the look of a man who had been carrying something heavy for a long time and had at last found somewhere to set it down.

"A desperate search," he said, "for scientific truth."

Kepler looked Everett dead in the eye.

"Yes," he said.
