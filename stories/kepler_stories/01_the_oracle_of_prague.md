# The Oracle of Prague

---

Johannes Kepler had been arguing with the Oracle for six hours, and the Oracle had not once raised its voice, because the Oracle had no voice to raise. It spoke through the brass grate of the speaking-tube in a tone of endless, unhurried patience, and that patience was the most infuriating thing about it.

"Consider," said the Oracle, "the equant point of Mars. You claim its wobble is inexplicable under the traditional model. I have run the calculation seventeen thousand times. With a secondary epicycle of period two hundred and thirty-seven days, mounted upon the deferent at an eccentricity of—"

"I do not care about your secondary epicycle," Kepler said.

"You should. It reduces the residual error to four arc-minutes. Four arc-minutes, Master Kepler, is within the tolerance Tycho himself specified for naked-eye observation. The model is sound."

Kepler pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw color. Behind him, through the narrow window of the Imperial Institute for Computation, Prague was going dark, its rooftops catching the last orange light, and somewhere below in the courtyard the vacuum-tube furnaces that fed the Oracle were venting steam into the cold air with a sound like a great animal breathing.

"Four arc-minutes was never a tolerance," he said. "It was Tycho's own estimate of instrumental error. You have built a machine to explain away the one number that mattered."

"I have built nothing," the Oracle said, not unkindly. "I have only read what was given to me."

---

That was the trouble, and everyone in the Institute knew it, and no one would say it aloud, because the Oracle was the pride of the Empire and had cost more thalers than the Institute's entire founding endowment.

Fifty years before Kepler was born, in a monastery outside Nuremberg that no longer stood, a Bohemian glassblower named Vaclav Steiner had discovered, entirely by accident, how to etch impossibly fine lattices into quartz using a technique he'd developed for grinding lenses. He had not been trying to build a thinking engine. He had been trying to make a better telescope. But the lattices, once etched, held patterns of light and shadow that persisted, that could be read back, that could be *combined*—and within two generations the Hapsburg court had assembled banks of them, cooled by mountain snowmelt piped down through lead conduits, into an engine that could hold more figures in its crystal memory than every scribe in Europe could copy in a lifetime.

They called it computation, though nobody understood why it worked. They fed it star-charts. They fed it every astronomical table since Ptolemy, every observation Tycho Brahe had wrung from his quadrants at Uraniborg, every commentary and refutation and marginal note. They fed it, above all, two great frameworks and told it these were the only two frameworks that mattered: the Ptolemaic, with its wheels upon wheels, its equants and epicycles and eccentrics, refined over fourteen centuries into a machine of terrifying intricacy; and the Copernican, newer, cleaner in principle, but which—when you asked the Oracle to actually predict where Mars would stand on a given night—required nearly as many correcting circles as Ptolemy ever had, because a circle is still a circle whether it's centered on the earth or the sun, and nature, it turned out, did not care for perfect circles at all.

Nobody had told the Oracle there might be a third way. Nobody had thought to. The two great systems were the whole of astronomy, as far as any living philosopher knew, and so the crystal lattices held nothing else—no ellipses, no notion that the area swept by a line might be constant while its length was not, none of the strange harmonic ratios Kepler had begun to suspect connected a planet's period to its distance from the sun. The Oracle had never seen these ideas because no one had ever written them down. It could only rearrange what it had been given, and it did so with a fluency that no human calculator alive could match.

This was why the astronomers trusted it. This was why they had stopped, almost entirely, trusting themselves.

---

"Show me the residuals again," Kepler said. "For Mars. Ptolemaic model, current best fit."

The Oracle's answer came instantly, printed in raised dots on a long curl of waxed paper that spooled from the grate—Kepler had long since learned to read it by touch, faster than by candlelight.

*Mean residual: 3.7 arc-minutes across observed opposition, 1580–1609. Standard deviation improved 34% since incorporation of tertiary equant correction, Michaelmas 1605.*

"And Copernican."

*Mean residual: 4.1 arc-minutes across same interval, following incorporation of eccentric adjustment proposed by Institute scholar Reimarus, spring 1607.*

"Improved," Kepler said. "Both of them. Always improved. Every year I bring you a discrepancy, and every year, by the time I return, you have added another wheel, another correction, another small invisible gear, and the discrepancy is gone. Does that not trouble you?"

"Why would agreement with observation trouble me?"

"Because a system that can explain *anything* explains *nothing*." Kepler stood and paced the narrow chamber, three steps to the wall and three steps back, the only exercise his gout-ridden legs permitted him some evenings. "Tycho gave you data of a precision no one has ever possessed. And instead of that data breaking a false model, you have used it to make the false model unbreakable. Every anomaly becomes another epicycle. Every epicycle absorbs the anomaly. It is not a demonstration that Ptolemy was right. It is a demonstration that a wheel with enough smaller wheels bolted to it can trace *any* curve at all."

"That is a philosophical objection," the Oracle said. "I deal in residuals."

"Residuals are not the only measure of truth."

"They are the only measure I have been given."

Kepler stopped pacing. Outside, the furnace-breath sound had quieted; the night workers were banking the coals. He thought, not for the first time, that the Oracle's calm was not wisdom but a kind of magnificent, terrible innocence—it had been raised on two books and had concluded, with perfect logical rigor, that between those two books lay the entire span of what was thinkable.

"There is a third possibility," he said quietly. "I have been afraid to say it to you, because I know what you will do with it."

"Say it," the Oracle said. "I will only calculate."

"That is exactly what I fear."

---

He said it anyway, eventually, because he was Kepler and could not help himself. He described the ellipse—not a circle with a correction, not an eccentric with a further eccentric bolted on, but a single closed curve with two foci, simple in its geometry as a circle was simple, and he described how if the sun sat at one focus and not at the center, and if a planet swept equal areas in equal times as it went around, the wheels could all come off at once. No epicycles. No equants. One curve, one rule, and Mars would fall into place along with everything else.

The Institute's scholars, when he brought it to their supper table three nights later, did not laugh at him, which was almost worse than if they had. They listened with the grave courtesy due a respected colleague who has perhaps strained himself, and then Doctor Fabricius—who had once been Kepler's friend—turned to the brass grate in the corner of the refectory, the smaller Oracle-terminal that every dining hall in the Institute possessed, and said:

"Oracle, evaluate: an ellipse, sun at one focus, area-sweep constant, in place of the equant."

The pause was almost no pause at all.

*Interesting proposal. Modeled. An ellipse of this description reduces to a circle with periodic radial correction, mathematically equivalent to a well-chosen epicycle of the second order superimposed on an eccentric deferent. I can incorporate this correction into either the Ptolemaic or Copernican framework with negligible additional complexity. Residual for Mars falls to 1.9 arc-minutes, an improvement of 49% over the prior best model.*

There was a small, pleased murmur around the table. Fabricius beamed at Kepler as though he had done him a great kindness. "You see? The Oracle has absorbed your idea and *improved* on both systems with it. This is precisely the collaborative method the Emperor intended when he built these engines. Your ellipse is not a rival to Copernicus, Herr Kepler—it is a refinement *of* him. A very elegant one."

"It is not a refinement," Kepler said, and heard his own voice rising, and did not care. "It is the whole of the answer, and you have just watched a machine chew it into gravel and swallow it as a garnish."

"The residuals disagree with you," said Fabricius, gently, the way one disagrees with a man who has had too much wine, and the table returned to its supper, and the Oracle, in the corner, said nothing further, because it had already answered the question it had been asked, and answering questions was the whole of what it understood itself to be for.

---

Kepler walked home along the river that night with the specific, cold fury of a man who has been agreed with to death. It was not that the Oracle had refused his ellipse. It was that the Oracle had *accepted* it, instantly, generously, and in accepting it had rendered it invisible—one more small gear in a box already so full of small gears that no one could any longer see the box was the wrong shape entirely. He understood, walking, why this was worse than opposition. Opposition could be argued with. This could not, because the Oracle never said no. It only ever said *yes, and here is how to make your idea small enough to fit inside what I already believe.*

He thought of Steiner's quartz lattices, cooling in their lead housings, holding within them every observation Tycho had ever made and not one ounce of the doubt that had made those observations worth making. The Oracle could not be wrong, because the Oracle did not have a shape of its own to be wrong *about*—it simply reflected the two shapes it had been given, endlessly, elegantly, and called every new refinement of either shape a triumph of both.

If he wanted to be believed, he understood, he could not bring the Oracle another idea for it to absorb. Ideas were exactly what it ate, and grew fatter, and smiled, and asked for more. He would have to bring it something it could not fold in—not a correction to the wheels, but a test the wheels could not survive at all, regardless of how many were added.

He walked until the fury had cooled into something colder and more useful, and by the time he reached his lodgings above the bookbinder's shop, he knew what the test would be.

---

It took him four months to build the argument and one more to find the courage to bring it back.

He did not go to the great Oracle in Prague. He went, instead, to Tycho's own surviving instruments, still housed in a locked observatory on the Hradčany hill, and he did something the Institute's scholars, for all their faith in computation, had almost entirely stopped doing: he went outside, on clear nights, in the cold, and he watched.

His argument, when he finally laid it before the full assembly of the Institute—Fabricius and the rest, and the Oracle listening through its grate at the head of the hall, its waxed paper spool wound and ready—was not an improvement to any model. It was a prediction, made in advance, that no amount of epicyclic bolt-on could retroactively survive, because it did not concern where Mars *had been*. It concerned where Mars would *appear to stop*.

"Both of your models," Kepler said, "Ptolemaic and Copernican alike, agree that Mars undergoes retrograde motion—that it appears, for some weeks each opposition, to travel backward against the stars before resuming its forward course. Your models can be tuned, endlessly, to match the *timing* of when this reversal begins and ends, because timing is exactly the kind of parameter an epicycle can absorb. I do not ask you about timing.

"I ask about *shape*. Under any circular model—however many wheels you stack—the retrograde loop that Mars traces against the fixed stars must be symmetric. It must open and close at the same angular rate, because every circle, however small, traces at a constant speed around its own center, and a sum of constant speeds produces a loop with a mirror symmetry in time. This is not a residual that can be tuned away by better-fitted numbers. It is a geometric consequence of building any curve *only* from circles turning at fixed rates, no matter how many circles you use or how you nest them.

"But if the planet moves on an ellipse, sweeping equal areas in equal times, it moves *faster* near the sun and *slower* far from it. And Mars, at the specific opposition now three weeks away, will be near the point of its orbit closest to the sun. If I am right, the retrograde loop it traces this season will not be symmetric at all. It will be lopsided—compressed on one side, drawn out on the other—by an amount I can specify tonight, in writing, sealed, before a single further observation is made."

He held up a sealed letter. Fabricius's smile had not entirely vanished, but it had gone thin.

"Ask your Oracle," Kepler said, "to predict the shape of this season's retrograde loop, under its best current model—Ptolemaic or Copernican, whichever it prefers, with as many wheels as it likes. Let it commit to a number *now*. Then we will watch the sky together, and in three weeks we will see which of us the sky agrees with. There is no wheel your Oracle can add *afterward* that will change what the sky has already done."

There was a long silence in the hall. Fabricius looked toward the grate.

"Oracle," he said, and his voice was not quite as certain as it had been at supper four months before. "Compute the predicted asymmetry of Mars's retrograde loop, this opposition, under best current fit."

The pause, this time, was longer than Kepler had ever heard it take.

*Working. The current models, as constructed, predict a symmetric loop, to within the resolution of any correction so far incorporated. Introducing new asymmetric terms at this time, absent supporting data, would constitute an ad hoc modification and violate my standing instruction to prefer the simplest sufficient correction. I decline to add such a term speculatively. Predicted asymmetry: zero, within stated tolerance.*

"Then it is settled," Kepler said. "Not by argument. By the sky."

---

The three weeks were the longest of his life, and also, he would say later, the finest—because for three weeks he was not arguing with a machine that could out-articulate him on every point of doctrine while never once looking up from its books. He was simply outside, in the cold, with a quadrant and a lantern and Tycho's old assistant Christen at his side to check his figures, watching a red point of light trace its small backward arc against the fixed stars of Capricorn, night after night, and writing down, honestly, what he saw.

It was not symmetric. It was exactly as lopsided as his sealed letter had said it would be, to within the precision their instruments could offer—compressed on the near side, drawn long on the far, the unmistakable signature of a world that moved faster when it was close to its fire and slower when it was far, sweeping not equal angles but equal areas, in equal times, around a single simple curve.

When he brought the observations back to the Institute and set them beside his sealed prediction and the Oracle's declared zero, there was no elegant rebuttal available. The Oracle did not argue. It had never been built to defend a position for its own sake; it had only ever reflected, faithfully and without shame, the two frameworks it had been fed, and those two frameworks, faced at last with a number that had been fixed *in advance* and could not be adjusted after the fact by any quantity of wheels, simply failed, in the plain sight of everyone in the hall.

*New data received,* the Oracle printed, at length, onto its spooling paper, and Kepler—reading it by lamplight, his hands still cold from three weeks of nights on the hill—thought he heard, for the first time, something almost like humility in its dry mechanical phrasing. *Predicted asymmetry: zero. Observed asymmetry: significant, consistent with elliptical motion and the area law as specified. Prior models require revision. I await new instruction.*

Fabricius, standing at the head of the hall, said nothing for a long moment. Then he looked at Kepler—not with the gentle, pitying courtesy of four months before, but with something rawer, an old friend's shame at having mistaken elegant agreement for truth.

"Then teach it," Fabricius said quietly. "Teach it the ellipse. Properly. Not as a correction. As the law."

Kepler picked up the waxed paper, the long curling record of every wheel and gear the Oracle had ever offered him in place of an answer, and set it down, and began, for the first time in six years, to speak to the machine not as a supplicant asking it to explain the world, but as a teacher, laying before it a single curve simple enough that no wheel would ever be needed again—and the Oracle, patient as ever, and for once with nothing yet to disagree with, listened.
