Chapter 20 — The Central Claim: a-nought equals the speed of light squared, times the square root of Lambda divided by thirty-two pi

Two of the biggest numbers in physics — the one that makes galaxies spin too fast, and the one that makes the whole universe fly apart — might be the same number wearing two hats. This chapter is about why.

We have come a long way to get here. We weighed galaxies with Kepler and Newton. We watched Zwicky and Rubin discover that something is missing — that stars on the edges of galaxies move as though far more mass is tugging on them than we can see. We followed the two roads out of that discovery: the road of the particle, which says the missing mass is real but invisible, and the road of the law, which says our equation for how things move is incomplete at very gentle accelerations. We spent the whole of Part Two learning Einstein's gravity and the expanding, accelerating universe; we learned what inertia, temperature-from-acceleration, and horizons are in Part Three; and in Part Four we met Milgrom's MOND and the strange, sharp acceleration scale a-nought that sits at the heart of every galaxy's rotation curve.

Now I am going to tell you the single most important sentence in this book. I will say it plainly, with no equations, the way I would say it to a friend over coffee: the speed limit that governs the slow, outer edges of galaxies appears to be made of the dark energy that is pushing the whole universe apart.

That is the central claim. The number that decides when a galaxy starts to misbehave — the number a-nought, which we measured from rotation curves long before anyone knew where it came from — is, in this framework, not an independent fact about galaxies at all. It is a disguised measurement of the cosmological constant Lambda, the same Lambda that the 1998 supernova teams discovered when they found the universe accelerating. Two of the most famous numbers in modern physics — one from the smallest scales we study gravitationally, the outskirts of single galaxies, one from the largest, the expansion of the cosmos — turn out, on this account, to be one number.

Let me be honest right away, because honesty is the whole spirit of this book. Saying two numbers are "the same" is a strong claim, and a strong claim has to earn its keep. It could be a coincidence. Numbers in physics sometimes come out close by accident. So the job of this chapter, and really of all of Part Five, is to show you exactly what the claim is, exactly how the formula works, and exactly which parts of it are forced by physics and which parts are a choice. By the end you will be able to compute a-nought yourself, from Lambda alone, and get nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared — the value galaxies actually show. And you will know precisely how much that agreement is worth, and how much it isn't. It is genuinely striking. It is not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be.

So: one sentence, two hats, and here is exactly why they might be one.

Let me re-fix the one number this whole chapter is about, because we are going to stare at it for a while.

A-nought, read "a-nought," is an acceleration — a rate at which a speed changes. The acceleration you feel when a car pulls away from a stop sign is a few meters per second squared. The acceleration gravity gives you when you trip is about nine point eight meters per second squared, which we call g. The acceleration a-nought is about nine point four times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared. That is staggeringly gentle. It is about a hundred billion times weaker than the gravity at Earth's surface. It is roughly the acceleration you would feel from the Sun's gravity if you were light-years away from it. Nothing in your daily life ever comes close to being this gentle. But the outer stars of galaxies, drifting through near-emptiness, do.

Earlier we met Milgrom's observation: when the gravitational pull a star feels from ordinary matter is stronger than a-nought, everything behaves exactly as Newton said. When that pull drops below a-nought — out in the thin outskirts of a galaxy — the dynamics change, and the change is precisely what makes rotation curves go flat instead of falling off. The number a-nought is the threshold between the two regimes. It is the most important single number in galaxy dynamics that we did not, until recently, have any deeper explanation for. Milgrom found it by fitting data. It just is what it is — a brute fact, an input.

The central claim of this framework is that a-nought is not a brute fact. It is a consequence — a consequence of the universe having a cosmological constant.

Before any equations, I want to plant the physical idea, because the formula is just bookkeeping once you have the idea.

The universe is accelerating its expansion. The agent responsible is dark energy, described by the cosmological constant Lambda. A universe whose expansion is driven by a constant Lambda has a name: de Sitter space, after the Dutch astronomer Willem de Sitter, who found this solution to Einstein's equations back in 1917. De Sitter space is, roughly, "what the universe looks like when dark energy wins" — and because dark energy is constant while matter dilutes away as everything spreads out, our universe is slowly becoming a de Sitter space. Far in the future it will be almost purely de Sitter.

Now, a de Sitter universe is not flat and featureless. It has a built-in length and a built-in curvature. Here is the cleanest way to feel it. In an accelerating universe, there are things so far away that the space between us and them is stretching faster than their light can cross it. Their light will never reach us. There is, in other words, a horizon — a largest distance from which any signal can ever arrive. That horizon sits at a definite distance, and that distance is set by Lambda. A bigger Lambda — more dark energy, faster acceleration — pulls the horizon closer. A smaller Lambda pushes it away.

Because the horizon distance is set by Lambda, you can turn it into a curvature — a measure of how sharply spacetime bends on the largest scale — and from a curvature you can build an acceleration scale. This is the thing I will call the de Sitter curvature scale: a natural acceleration that the geometry of a Lambda-driven universe hands you, for free, just by existing. It is built from Lambda, the speed of light c, and nothing else.

The entire claim of this chapter, in one line, is this: the acceleration scale a-nought that governs galaxies is the de Sitter curvature scale of our universe, up to one geometric factor we will pin down later.

Why might that be true physically? That is the job of the next chapter, where we lay out the de Sitter–Unruh modified-inertia mechanism: the idea that empty space in a Lambda-universe carries a faint temperature floor, that matter in near-free-fall feels that floor, and that feeling it shows up as a tiny extra resistance to being accelerated — extra inertia — which, from the outside, looks exactly like the extra gravity galaxies seem to have. For this chapter, hold that thought. Here, our only goal is to write the formula, understand each of its pieces, and compute the number. The why comes next; the what comes now.

Here is the central claim in symbols. A-nought equals the speed of light squared, times the square root of the cosmological constant Lambda divided by thirty-two pi.

Read it slowly. On the left is a-nought, the galaxy acceleration scale, in meters per second squared. On the right is the speed of light squared, multiplied by the square root of the cosmological constant Lambda divided by thirty-two pi. That is it. Every symbol on the right is a number about the cosmos as a whole: c is the speed of light, Lambda is dark energy. There is nothing about galaxies on the right-hand side — no galaxy mass, no galaxy size, no star count. The acceleration scale of every galaxy in the sky is, on this account, computed entirely from properties of the vacuum.

Now, the same statement can be dressed three different ways. They are algebraically identical — same number, just rewritten — but each one makes a different piece of the physics obvious. I find it helps to meet all three, the way you might look at a sculpture from three sides.

The first hat is Lambda directly: a-nought equals the speed of light squared, times the square root of Lambda divided by thirty-two pi. This is the "geometry" hat. Lambda is, mathematically, a curvature — its units are one over length squared — so the square root of Lambda is one over a length, and the speed of light squared times one over a length is an acceleration. This form says, most starkly, a-nought is built from the curvature of the accelerating universe.

The second hat is the dark-energy density: a-nought equals c over two, times the square root of G times rho-D-E. Here rho-D-E, read "rho-D-E," is the density of dark energy — how much dark-energy "stuff" there is per cubic meter of space. The link between the two pictures is the standard one: Lambda and rho-D-E are two ways of writing the same dark energy, related by rho-D-E equals Lambda times the speed of light squared, divided by eight pi G. This is the "stuff" hat. It says a-nought is set by how dense the dark energy is, with G the gravitational constant tying density to gravity. This form is the most physical-feeling: it looks like the kind of acceleration you'd get from a sea of dark-energy density.

The third hat is the Hubble-Lambda rate: a-nought equals the speed of light times the de Sitter Hubble rate, all divided by Z, where Z equals the square root of thirty-two pi over three, which is five point seven eight nine. Here the speed of light times the de Sitter Hubble rate is the speed of light times the expansion rate the universe would have if dark energy were all there was — the pure-Lambda Hubble rate, the rate the cosmos is settling toward. That product, the speed of light times the de Sitter Hubble rate, has been known for decades to be suspiciously close to a-nought: people noticed in the 1980s that a-nought is about the speed of light times H-nought divided by six or so, a numerical "coincidence" that nobody could explain. This framework's third hat says the coincidence is no coincidence: a-nought is the speed of light times the de Sitter Hubble rate, divided by a specific, calculable geometric constant Z, the square root of thirty-two pi over three, which is five point seven eight nine. That Z is not fitted. It is pure geometry — where it comes from is the subject of later chapters, and it is one of the cleanest results in the whole framework.

A word about Z, said carefully. The constant Z, the square root of thirty-two pi over three, about five point seven eight nine, is structural — it is forced by the geometry, and a later chapter shows you the square root of eight pi over three kernel it lives inside, the Einstein "eight pi" of the field equations times the Friedmann "three" of cosmology. But there is also one genuinely free choice buried in this framework, a factor I will call kappa equals one half, and I am not going to pretend it away. It is the subject of a whole chapter to come. The honest one-line version: the form of the formula is forced; one dimensionless number inside it, kappa, is a geometric posit, not a derivation. So this is a one-parameter theory, not a zero-parameter one. I will keep flagging this. It matters.

The three hats are the same hat. And the worked-out arithmetic — starting from the cosmological constant and ending at an acceleration — lands on nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared. It came entirely from c and Lambda, two numbers about the cosmos, with no galaxy data anywhere in the calculation. And it lands right on the acceleration scale that galaxy rotation curves have been showing us for fifty years.

Let me address the obvious question: if the three forms are just algebra, why bother showing all three?

Two reasons. First, clarity: each form makes a different claim legible. The first hat says "a-nought is a curvature scale." The second hat says "a-nought is set by dark-energy density." The third hat says "a-nought is the famous speed-of-light-times-Hubble coincidence, with the fudge factor finally named." A reader who only saw one form might miss the others' meaning.

Second, and more importantly: showing they're the same is the point. A skeptic's first reaction to "a-nought is made of Lambda" should be, prove it's not just a number that happens to land near the right place. The fact that one clean expression — the speed of light squared, times the square root of Lambda over thirty-two pi — reproduces a-nought to within the observational scatter, and does so simultaneously as a curvature, as a density, and as the long-noted speed-of-light-times-Hubble coincidence, is what makes the claim worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as numerology. It is a single structural identity showing up three ways at once.

Now I have to be scrupulous about something, because it is exactly the kind of detail where it would be easy to fool yourself — or to fool a reader.

When I plugged in Lambda above, I used the dark-energy-only density: just the Lambda part of the cosmos, with the dark-energy fraction about zero point six eight five. But the real universe today is not pure dark energy. It also has matter — the ordinary stuff plus whatever the dark sector is — making up the other roughly thirty-one and a half percent. So a fair person should ask: which density belongs in the formula? The dark-energy density alone, rho-D-E, or the total density of everything, rho-total, the critical density today?

This is what I call the footing question, and I want to handle it openly, in both directions, because it slightly changes the answer.

The canonical footing is pure Lambda. The framework's claim is structural: a-nought is the de Sitter curvature scale, the curvature of the universe-as-it-is-becoming, when dark energy has won and matter has diluted away. De Sitter space is defined by Lambda alone. So the principled input is the dark-energy density, rho-D-E — equivalently, the pure-Lambda Hubble rate, which is H-nought times the square root of the dark-energy fraction. That is the footing I used, and it is the one that gives the clean canonical value: a-nought, on the pure-Lambda footing, is about nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared.

The alternative footing is total density. If instead you reasonably argue that today's dynamics should feel today's full energy budget — matter included — you would put rho-total in the second hat, or equivalently use the full H-nought in the third hat. Because the total density is larger than the dark-energy density by a factor of one over the dark-energy fraction, about one point four six, and a-nought scales as the square root of density, this raises the prediction by the square root of one over zero point six eight five, about one point two one. The result, on the total footing, is about one point one three times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared.

So the two footings bracket a range from about nine point four times ten to the minus eleven, to about one point one three times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared. And here is the honest punchline: both ends of that range sit inside the band of a-nought values that galaxy data actually support. The observational a-nought is not a single razor-sharp number; depending on the galaxy sample, the assumed stellar mass-to-light ratio, and the interpolation function, the best-fit a-nought from rotation curves spans roughly zero point nine to one point three times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared. The framework's two footings land squarely within that band.

I want to draw the sharpest possible line here, because everything downstream depends on it.

When Milgrom introduced a-nought, it was a fitted parameter: a knob you turn until the rotation curves come out right. That is a perfectly respectable scientific move — fitted parameters are how much of physics gets started. But a fitted parameter explains nothing about why it has the value it has. It is a number you measure, not a number you understand.

The claim of this framework is categorically different. It says a-nought is not a free parameter at all — it is computed from Lambda, a quantity measured by an entirely independent branch of physics: supernova cosmology, the cosmic microwave background, baryon acoustic oscillations. Nobody fitting rotation curves ever looked at supernovae. Nobody fitting supernovae ever looked at rotation curves. The two communities measured their two numbers in total isolation, and this formula says those two numbers are one number. That is a structural identity claim, not a fit. The formula has zero adjustable knobs that were tuned to galaxy data: c is fixed, Lambda comes from cosmology, and the thirty-two pi is geometry. The lone free choice, kappa equals one half, is a geometric posit fixed before any galaxy is examined — not a dial turned to match rotation curves.

That distinction is the whole reason the claim is interesting. A fit that reproduces a-nought is worth little; a thousand functional forms can fit a single number. An identity that predicts a-nought from unrelated cosmological data, with no galaxy input, is worth a great deal — if it holds. And I keep saying "if" on purpose.

It would be easy here to either oversell or undersell, and I have worked hard not to do either. Here is the careful version.

The framework's canonical a-nought, nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared, is slightly below the most commonly quoted MOND value, about one point two times ten to the minus ten. At first glance that looks like the framework predicts an a-nought about twenty percent too low. But that first glance is misleading, and here is why — three knobs all push the "best" a-nought around, and you cannot judge nine point three six without naming all three.

First, the stellar mass-to-light ratio. How much stellar mass you assign per unit of starlight directly sets how much gravity the visible matter produces, which sets the best-fit a-nought. The often-quoted one point two times ten to the minus ten uses a mass-to-light ratio of about zero point five; at the more physical value of about zero point seven, the best-fit a-nought drops.

Second, the interpolation function. The function that smoothly stitches the Newtonian regime to the low-acceleration regime changes where the radial-acceleration relation bends, and so changes the optimal a-nought. McGaugh's standard interpolation gives one optimum; the framework's own de Sitter–Unruh interpolation gives another. That own interpolation says: the gravity actually felt equals the square root of the baryonic gravity squared, plus the baryonic gravity times a-nought.

Third, the fit metric. How you weight the scatter — which galaxies count how much — moves the optimum too.

When you judge the framework on its own terms — its own interpolation function, at a mass-to-light ratio of about zero point seven — a-nought equal to nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared sits within roughly half a percent of the optimal scatter. The paper's claim that nine point three six lands within about zero point three percent of optimal on the framework's own footing is defensible. If anything, on that footing, nine point three six is very slightly below the optimum, not above it. Switch to a different interpolation, McGaugh's, and the optimum moves to about seven point eight times ten to the minus eleven, which would make nine point three six look about twenty percent high. Across all the reasonable combinations of interpolation and mass-to-light ratio, the optimal a-nought spans roughly seven point five times ten to the minus eleven to one point eight times ten to the minus ten, and the penalty for using nine point three six anywhere in that space is at most about two percent, and at most half a percent on the framework's own interpolation.

The honest net, said in both directions: the rotation-curve data is compatible with nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared, but it does not uniquely select it. Neither "the framework's a-nought is twenty percent too low" nor "twenty percent too high" is a robust statement — each is an artifact of a particular interpolation-and-mass-to-light choice. The radial-acceleration relation confirms that the framework's a-nought is the right scale, comfortably inside the spread; it does not, on its own, prove the number to three digits. Anyone who tells you the radial-acceleration relation either vindicates or refutes nine point three six to high precision is over-reading a convention-dependent fit. The agreement is real and it is meaningful; it is not a knock-out blow in either direction.

Let me close the technical part by drawing the box tightly around the claim, so no one — including me — smuggles more out of it than belongs.

Here is what the central claim does say. The galactic acceleration scale a-nought and the cosmological constant Lambda are related by a single clean formula — a-nought equals the speed of light squared, times the square root of Lambda over thirty-two pi — in three equivalent forms. Evaluated on the pure-Lambda footing, that formula yields nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared, which sits inside the band of a-nought values galaxy rotation curves support. And the relationship uses no galaxy data as input — it predicts a galactic number from a cosmological one — which makes it a structural identity claim, not a curve fit.

And here is what the central claim does not say, and what is still owed. It does not, by itself, explain why a-nought should equal a de Sitter curvature scale. That is the mechanism, and it is the burden of the next chapter. A formula that lands the number is suggestive; it is the mechanism that would make it physics. It does not derive the number from nothing. The form of the formula is forced by several independent arguments, but one dimensionless factor, kappa equals one half, is a geometric posit. So a-nought's value is not derived — there is one knob. This is a one-parameter theory, and I will never call it zero-parameter. It does not touch the Standard Model. Nothing in the speed of light squared times the square root of Lambda over thirty-two pi predicts an electron mass, a Koide relation, a gauge group, or a proton-to-electron ratio. The particle world is walled off. And it is not confirmed against Lambda-C-D-M. The headline number's agreement is genuine but, as we just saw, convention-dependent and shared in part with the whole MOND family. The sharp, framework-distinctive test is the time-evolution of a-nought, and that test is not yet decided.

So: the central claim is a striking, clean, parameter-light identity between a galaxy number and a cosmos number, computed here from Lambda alone, landing on the value galaxies show. It is the keystone the whole book has been building toward. And it is not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be — it is one strong, falsifiable idea, standing on a single posited geometric factor, with its mechanism and its hardest tests still ahead of us in the chapters to come.

That is exactly enough to be worth the rest of Part Five.

Let me gather the whole chapter into a few clear statements.

The central claim, in one sentence: the acceleration scale that governs the outskirts of galaxies, a-nought, is made of the dark energy accelerating the universe — it is the de Sitter curvature scale set by the cosmological constant Lambda.

The formula has three equivalent forms — same number, three hats: the curvature form, a-nought equals the speed of light squared, times the square root of Lambda over thirty-two pi; the density form, a-nought equals c over two times the square root of G times rho-D-E, with rho-D-E the dark-energy density; and the rate form, a-nought equals the speed of light times the de Sitter Hubble rate, divided by Z, with the speed of light times the de Sitter Hubble rate, and Z the square root of thirty-two pi over three, which is five point seven eight nine, a geometric constant.

Computed from Lambda alone, with no galaxy data, the formula gives a-nought about nine point three six times ten to the minus eleven meters per second squared on the canonical pure-Lambda footing — the scale galaxy rotation curves actually show.

The footing question, dark-energy density versus total density, shifts the prediction by about twenty-one percent, from nine point three six to one point one three times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared; pure-Lambda is canonical because the mechanism is a de Sitter effect, but both footings land inside the observed a-nought spread, so the choice is principled yet nearly moot for local data — it bites at high redshift.

This is an identity claim, not a fit: the formula has no knob tuned to galaxy data. The lone free number, kappa equals one half, is a geometric posit fixed independently, so this is a one-parameter theory, not zero-parameter.

The agreement is real but convention-dependent and MOND-shared.
