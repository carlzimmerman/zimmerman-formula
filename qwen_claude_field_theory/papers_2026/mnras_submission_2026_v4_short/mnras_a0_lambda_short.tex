\begin{abstract}
Galaxies spin as if they feel more pull than their visible matter can supply. The extra pull appears below one special acceleration, $a_0\simeq1.2\times10^{-10}$ m s$^{-2}$. This number is close to $\tfrac12c\sqrt{G\rho_\Lambda}$, where $\rho_\Lambda$ is the density of the cosmological constant (dark energy). We call the guess $a_0=\kappa\,c\sqrt{G\rho_\Lambda}$, with $\kappa$ a pure number, the Crispy Fried Chicken conjecture, and test it with every step of the algebra shown. First, what is $\kappa$? On the SPARC galaxy sample three methods give $0.45\pm0.07$, $0.58\pm0.18$ and $0.43\pm0.08$. All agree with $1/2$, and with other simple numbers. The answer depends on how much mass each unit of starlight carries, so $1/2$ is a fitted value, not a derived one. Second, did $a_0$ change over cosmic time? If it is tied to dark energy, it did not. If it is tied to the expansion rate, it was 3.8 times larger at redshift $z=2.5$. Dark-matter ($\Lambda$CDM) haloes predict about 1.6 times. Working out $a_0$ from one galaxy magnifies the error in its visible mass by 4 or more at the accelerations of today's distant galaxies, so they cannot decide. About eight magnified (lensed) discs at $z\simeq2.5$ would give 20:1 odds if their mass errors were independent. If the errors share a common calibration error of 0.06 dex (15 per cent), 34 are needed, and above that the sample average alone can never reach 20:1.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Stars and gas in a spinning galaxy are pulled toward its centre with acceleration $g_{\rm obs}=V^2/R$, where $V$ is their speed at distance $R$ from the centre. The visible stars and gas alone would pull less; call that smaller value $g_{\rm bar}$ (``bar'' is for baryons, meaning ordinary matter). Across thousands of measurements $g_{\rm obs}$ follows one curve of $g_{\rm bar}$ \citep{McGaugh2016,Lelli2017}. The curve has one built-in constant, an acceleration $a_0\simeq1.2\times10^{-10}$ m s$^{-2}$. Where $g_{\rm bar}\gg a_0$, $g_{\rm obs}=g_{\rm bar}$. Where $g_{\rm bar}\ll a_0$, $g_{\rm obs}\simeq\sqrt{g_{\rm bar}a_0}$. \citet{Milgrom1983} proposed this as a modified law of motion (MOND) and noted that $a_0$ is about $cH_0$, where $c$ is the speed of light and $H_0$ is how fast the universe expands today.

That coincidence can be read three ways. (i) $a_0$ follows the expansion rate $H(z)$, where redshift $z$ measures how far back in time we look; then $a_0$ was larger in the past. (ii) $a_0$ follows the cosmological constant, which never changes; then $a_0$ is constant. (iii) There is no basic $a_0$, and in $\Lambda$CDM the curve emerges from dark-matter haloes \citep{Navarro2017}, whose pull also grows with redshift. Today (i) and (ii) differ by only 21 per cent, too little to measure. At $z=2.5$ they differ by a factor 3.8.

We test reading (ii), written as
\begin{equation}
a_0=\kappa\,c\sqrt{G\rho_\Lambda}.
\label{eq:relation}
\end{equation}
Here $G$ is Newton's constant, $\rho_\Lambda$ is the mass density of the cosmological constant and $\kappa$ is a pure number. We call this the Crispy Fried Chicken conjecture; the name is the author's nickname for it and carries no physics. We size differences in \emph{dex}: 0.3 dex is a factor 2, 1 dex a factor 10, and 0.06 dex is 15 per cent. We ask what $\kappa$ is (Section~\ref{sec:kappa}), and what it would take to test whether $a_0$ changes with redshift (Section~\ref{sec:ztest}). The idea is old \citep{Milgrom1999,Milgrom2020}, and \citet{Limbach2008} ran a redshift test up to $z=1.2$; we claim no priority. We do not derive $\kappa$.

\section{The conjecture, one step at a time}
\label{sec:relation}
\emph{Why the form is forced.} Suppose an acceleration is built from $c$, $G$ and a density $\rho$ as $a=c^{\alpha}G^{\beta}\rho^{\gamma}$. In kilograms (kg), metres (m) and seconds (s), $c$ is m s$^{-1}$, $G$ is m$^3$ kg$^{-1}$ s$^{-2}$, $\rho$ is kg m$^{-3}$ and an acceleration is m s$^{-2}$. The power of each unit must match on both sides:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{kg:}\quad & -\beta+\gamma=0,\\
\text{m:}\quad & \alpha+3\beta-3\gamma=1,\\
\text{s:}\quad & -\alpha-2\beta=-2 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dims}
\end{equation}
The first line gives $\gamma=\beta$. Putting that into the second gives $\alpha=1$. Putting $\alpha=1$ into the third gives $\beta=1/2$. So $a=\kappa c\sqrt{G\rho}$ is the only possible form, and units cannot fix $\kappa$. Choosing $\rho=\rho_\Lambda$ is the conjecture.

\emph{The density of dark energy.} For a flat universe $H^2=8\pi G\rho/3$. Today the total density is the critical density, and the part of it that is the cosmological constant is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\rm crit}=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G},\qquad \rho_\Lambda=\Omega_\Lambda\,\rho_{\rm crit}.
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Unlike matter, $\rho_\Lambda$ does not thin out as the universe expands. We use the \citet{Planck2020} values $H_0=67.4$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$ and $\Omega_\Lambda=0.685$, with 1 Mpc $=3.08568\times10^{22}$ m. Then equation~(\ref{eq:relation}) works out step by step as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
H_0&=\frac{67.4\times10^{3}}{3.08568\times10^{22}}=2.184\times10^{-18}\ {\rm s^{-1}},\\
\rho_{\rm crit}&=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}=8.533\times10^{-27}\ {\rm kg\,m^{-3}},\\
\rho_\Lambda&=0.685\,\rho_{\rm crit}=5.845\times10^{-27}\ {\rm kg\,m^{-3}},\\
\sqrt{G\rho_\Lambda}&=6.246\times10^{-19}\ {\rm s^{-1}},\\
c\sqrt{G\rho_\Lambda}&=1.872\times10^{-10}\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For $\kappa=1/2$ this gives $a_0=9.36\times10^{-11}$ m s$^{-2}$. Using $\rho_{\rm crit}$ instead of $\rho_\Lambda$ makes it larger by $\Omega_\Lambda^{-1/2}=1.208$, which is $1.13\times10^{-10}$ m s$^{-2}$. Both lie inside the stated uncertainty of the usual value, $(1.20\pm0.02\pm0.24)\times10^{-10}$ m s$^{-2}$ \citep{McGaugh2016}.

\section{Measuring $\kappa$}
\label{sec:kappa}
\emph{The curve.} We use the SPARC database \citep{Lelli2016}: 175 disc galaxies, of which we keep 2803 points in 164 galaxies with velocity errors below 10 per cent. The curve, in the form of \citet{McGaugh2016}, is
\begin{equation}
g_{\rm obs}=\nu(y)\,g_{\rm bar},\quad \nu(y)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\sqrt y}},\quad y\equiv\frac{g_{\rm bar}}{a_0}.
\label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}
For $y\gg1$, $e^{-\sqrt y}\to0$, so $\nu\to1$ and $g_{\rm obs}=g_{\rm bar}$. For $y\ll1$, write $s=\sqrt y$ and expand $1-e^{-s}=s-s^2/2+\dots=s(1-s/2+\dots)$. Then $\nu=(1/s)(1+s/2+\dots)$ and
\begin{equation}
g_{\rm obs}=\frac{g_{\rm bar}}{\sqrt y}\Big(1+\tfrac12\sqrt y\Big)=\sqrt{g_{\rm bar}a_0}\,\Big(1+\tfrac12\sqrt y\Big),
\label{eq:deep}
\end{equation}
because $g_{\rm bar}/\sqrt y=g_{\rm bar}\sqrt{a_0/g_{\rm bar}}=\sqrt{g_{\rm bar}a_0}$. The leading term says $a_0=g_{\rm obs}^2/g_{\rm bar}$.

\emph{Why $\kappa$ depends on the stars.} The pull $g_{\rm obs}=V^2/R$ is measured. But $g_{\rm bar}$ is computed from the light of the stars and the gas, and the stellar part is proportional to $\Upsilon$, the mass each unit of starlight carries, which comes from stellar models and is not measured. Where stars dominate, $g_{\rm bar}\propto\Upsilon$, so $a_0=g_{\rm obs}^2/g_{\rm bar}\propto1/\Upsilon$. Fitting equation~(\ref{eq:nu}) to SPARC gives $a_0=1.05$, $0.86$ and $0.71\times10^{-10}$ m s$^{-2}$, that is $\kappa=0.560$, $0.457$ and $0.381$, for $\Upsilon=0.5$, $0.6$ and $0.7$. Going from $\Upsilon=0.5$ to $0.6$ multiplies $\kappa$ by $0.457/0.560=0.82$, close to $0.5/0.6=0.83$. Stellar models give $\Upsilon=0.5$--$0.7$ \citep{Meidt2014}, and $\kappa=1/2$ needs $\Upsilon=0.555$. The scatter hardly changes (0.143--0.145 dex), so the data do not choose $\Upsilon$. One more choice matters: SPARC places 97 galaxies at distances computed with $H_0=73$, while $\rho_\Lambda$ uses 67.4. We rescale those distances to 67.4 so both sides of equation~(\ref{eq:relation}) use one $H_0$.

\emph{Three methods.} To loosen the grip of $\Upsilon$ we use three methods that depend on it differently. A: the rule that a galaxy's flat rotation speed $V_f$ obeys $V_f^4\simeq GM_ba_0$ ($M_b$ is the visible mass), corrected for galaxies that are not infinitely deep, for 24 deep galaxies. B: only the shape of the curve, where it bends, with a free vertical shift so that a wrong distance scale cannot matter. C: all 3391 points, letting each galaxy choose its own $\Upsilon$. They give $\kappa=0.450\pm0.073$, $0.58\pm0.18$ and $0.43\pm0.08$ (total errors). Each agrees with $\kappa=1/2$ (0.7, 0.5 and $0.9\sigma$ away) and with Milgrom's $a_0=cH_\Lambda/2\pi$, which has $\kappa=0.461$ (0.1, 0.7 and $0.4\sigma$), where $H_\Lambda=H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}$. Against this, the study of \citet{Desmond2023}, which also fits distances and inclinations, gives $\kappa=0.636\pm0.053$, $2.6\sigma$ above $1/2$. Changing the $H_0$ choice moves our three values by 0.05 to 0.10.

\emph{$\kappa$ cannot be separated from $H_0$.} Equation~(\ref{eq:rho}) gives $\sqrt{G\rho_\Lambda}=H_0\sqrt{3\Omega_\Lambda/8\pi}$, so $a_0=\kappa\,cH_0\sqrt{3\Omega_\Lambda/8\pi}$: only the product $\kappa H_0$ matters. The two current values $H_0=67.4$ and $73.04$ \citep{Riess2022} have the ratio $0.923$, and $0.4607/0.500=0.921$, so $(\kappa,H_0)=(0.500,67.4)$ and $(0.461,73.04)$ predict the same $a_0$ to $0.2$ per cent. We use $\kappa=1/2$ as a working value because it is the simplest number that fits, and for no deeper reason.

\section{Did $a_0$ change over cosmic time?}
\label{sec:ztest}
\subsection{Three predictions}
\label{sec:laws}
Let $\Delta(z)\equiv\log_{10}[a_0(z)/a_0(0)]$, the change since today in dex. \emph{(a) Tied to dark energy:} $\rho_\Lambda$ does not change, so $\Delta=0$. Plain MOND predicts the same; the dark-energy link adds a reason for it. \emph{(b) Tied to the critical density:} with $\rho_{\rm crit}(z)=3H^2(z)/8\pi G$ we get $a_0\propto H(z)$ and $\Delta=\log_{10}E(z)$, where $E(z)=H(z)/H_0=\sqrt{\Omega_m(1+z)^3+\Omega_\Lambda}$ and $\Omega_m=0.315$ is the matter share. At $z=2.5$,
\begin{equation}
E^2=0.315\times3.5^3+0.685=14.19,
\end{equation}
so $E=3.77$ and $\Delta=\log_{10}3.77=+0.58$. \emph{(c) Dark-matter haloes ($\Lambda$CDM):} the pull at the centre of a halo of mass $M$ and concentration $c_{200}$ is $g_{\max}\propto(GM)^{1/3}H^{4/3}c_{200}^2/f(c_{200})$, with $f(x)=\ln(1+x)-x/(1+x)$ (derivation: Appendix of \citealt{Zimmerman2026long}). At fixed $M$ the factor $H^{4/3}$ gives $\tfrac43\log_{10}E=+0.77$ at $z=2.5$, because haloes were denser; but older haloes were less concentrated, which subtracts $0.44$ \citep{DuttonMaccio2014}. The sum is $+0.33$ for $10^{12}\,{\rm M_\odot}$ and $+0.22$ for the lighter haloes of the discs of Section~\ref{sec:design}; across halo mass and model \citep{Duffy2008}, $+0.17$ to $+0.56$.

\subsection{Why one galaxy tells you so little}
\label{sec:amp}
Take one galaxy at one radius. Solve $\nu(y)=g_{\rm obs}/g_{\rm bar}$ for $y$, and set $\hat a_0=g_{\rm bar}/y$. How do measurement errors reach $\hat a_0$?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Take the logarithm of $g_{\rm obs}=\nu(y)g_{\rm bar}$: $\ln\nu(y)=\ln g_{\rm obs}-\ln g_{\rm bar}$.
\item Make small changes. With $n\equiv{\rm d}\ln\nu/{\rm d}\ln y$: $n\,{\rm d}\ln y={\rm d}\ln g_{\rm obs}-{\rm d}\ln g_{\rm bar}$.
\item From $\hat a_0=g_{\rm bar}/y$: ${\rm d}\ln\hat a_0={\rm d}\ln g_{\rm bar}-{\rm d}\ln y$.
\item Put ${\rm d}\ln y$ from step 2 into step 3:
\begin{equation}
{\rm d}\ln\hat a_0=-\frac1n\,{\rm d}\ln g_{\rm obs}+\Big(1+\frac1n\Big){\rm d}\ln g_{\rm bar}.
\label{eq:amp}
\end{equation}
\item For equation~(\ref{eq:nu}), $\ln\nu=-\ln(1-e^{-s})$ with $s=\sqrt y$, so ${\rm d}\ln\nu/{\rm d}s=-1/(e^{s}-1)$. With ${\rm d}\ln y=2\,{\rm d}s/s$,
\begin{equation}
n=-\frac{\sqrt y/2}{e^{\sqrt y}-1}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Here $n<0$, so define $A_{\rm obs}=1/|n|$ and $A_{\rm bar}=A_{\rm obs}-1$. A fractional error $\epsilon$ in $g_{\rm obs}$ becomes $A_{\rm obs}\epsilon$ in $\hat a_0$, and one in $g_{\rm bar}$ becomes $A_{\rm bar}\epsilon$. For small $y$, $n\to-1/2$, so $A_{\rm obs}=2$ and $A_{\rm bar}=1$. Both grow with $y$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:laws}b): $A_{\rm bar}=1.1$ at $y=0.01$, $1.7$ at $y=0.3$, $3.8$ at $y=2.4$ and $8.1$ at $y=6.4$. So errors are least harmful at low acceleration.

\subsection{What existing data say}
The largest uniform sample of distant galaxies, RC100 \citep[100 discs, $0.6<z<2.5$]{NestorShachar2023}, takes visible masses from starlight plus gas estimated from scaling relations \citep{Tacconi2018}. Its median is $y=2.4$ and its central 68 per cent lies in $0.9<y<7.2$. At the median $A_{\rm bar}=3.8$, so a typical $0.2$ dex error in visible mass becomes $0.76$ dex in $a_0$, larger than any gap in Fig.~\ref{fig:laws}a. For 41 of the 100 discs the visible matter alone already supplies the observed pull, so no $a_0$ can be worked out (27 per cent of discs at $z<1$, 51 per cent at $z>2$). RC100 therefore measures how well visible masses are known, not $a_0$. The rise at $z\simeq1$ reported by \citet{Ciocan2026} appears when disc masses are fitted together with a dark halo and not when they come from starlight (\citealt{Zimmerman2026long}, Sect.~4.3), so we count it neither way. No existing sample decides.

\subsection{What would decide}
\label{sec:design}
\emph{The gate.} Use only discs whose visible pull at their outermost radius is $g_{\rm bar}<0.3a_0$, where $A_{\rm obs}\le2.7$ and $A_{\rm bar}\le1.7$. With $g_{\rm bar}=\Gamma GM_b/R^2$ and a shape factor $\Gamma\simeq1.2$, this limit on the visible mass $M_b$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
M_b&<\frac{0.3\,a_0R^2}{\Gamma G}=\frac{0.3\,(9.36\times10^{-11})(3.086\times10^{19})^2}{1.2\,(6.674\times10^{-11})}\ {\rm kg}\\
&=1.7\times10^{8}\,{\rm M_\odot}\,(R/{\rm kpc})^2,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
using 1 kpc $=3.086\times10^{19}$ m and ${\rm M_\odot}=1.989\times10^{30}$ kg. At $z\simeq2.5$ such discs can be resolved only when a foreground galaxy magnifies them (gravitational lensing), and their gas mass must be measured, not guessed.

\emph{How many galaxies?} Each galaxy gives one value $\Delta_i$. We compare two predictions, $0$ and $\Delta$. For a Gaussian error $\sigma$ the log of the likelihood ratio (how much better one prediction fits) is $[x^2-(x-\Delta)^2]/2\sigma^2=(2x\Delta-\Delta^2)/2\sigma^2$. If $\Delta$ is true, the average of $x$ is $\Delta$, so the expected value is $\langle\ln B\rangle=\Delta^2/2\sigma^2$, and for $N$ independent galaxies $N\Delta^2/2\sigma^2$. Odds of 20:1 need $N\Delta^2/2\sigma^2=\ln20$, that is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}\le\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2\ln20}}=\frac{\Delta}{2.45}.
\end{equation}
So the sample mean must be known to $0.216/2.45=0.088$ dex to test against the $\Lambda$CDM rise of the gated discs, and to $0.576/2.45=0.24$ dex against $H(z)$. A fuller count notes that the two predictions also differ in spread: $s_0^2=\sigma_{\rm m}^2+\sigma_{\rm int}^2$ if $a_0$ is constant, and $s_1^2=s_0^2+\sigma_{\rm h}^2$ for haloes. Here $\sigma_{\rm m}$ is the measurement error, $\sigma_{\rm int}=0.034A_{\rm obs}(0.3)=0.09$ dex is the natural scatter of the curve, and $\sigma_{\rm h}=0.135$ dex is the scatter between haloes. If $a_0$ is constant, each galaxy then adds
\begin{equation}
\ln\frac{s_1}{s_0}-\frac12+\frac{s_0^2+\Delta^2}{2s_1^2}
\end{equation}
to $\langle\ln B\rangle$ (the less favourable case; if the haloes are right it is larger, 0.513 below). For $\sigma_{\rm m}=0.20$ dex, $s_0=0.2195$ and $s_1=0.2575$, so each galaxy adds $0.375$: eight galaxies give $e^{8\times0.375}=20:1$ and seven give $14:1$. Expected odds are not the chance of reaching them. By simulation that chance is 0.53 for eight galaxies, and 20 are needed for 0.9.

\emph{A shared calibration error does not average down.} Part of the mass error is the same for every galaxy (the conversion from CO light to gas mass, the stellar-mass zero point). A shared error $\delta_c$ (in dex) adds the same $\sigma_C=A_{\rm bar}\delta_c$ to every $\Delta_i$, so the variance of the sample mean is $s^2/N+\sigma_C^2$, which stops falling at $\sigma_C^2$. Using $\sigma_C$ in place of $\sigma/\sqrt N$ above, odds of 20:1 from the means need $\delta_c\le\Delta/(2.45\,A_{\rm bar})$. At $y=0.2$, $A_{\rm bar}=1.52$, so
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\delta_c&\le\frac{0.216}{2.45\times1.52}=0.058\ \text{dex (haloes)},\\
\delta_c&\le\frac{0.576}{2.45\times1.52}=0.155\ \text{dex ($H(z)$)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Beyond these limits the sample average alone can never reach 20:1, however many galaxies are used. (The spread of the sample also differs between the two predictions and adds some information: at $\delta_c=0.07$ dex, 52 galaxies would give 20:1.) Below the limits the number needed rises, from eight at $\delta_c=0$ to 14, 20 and 34 at $\delta_c=0.04$, $0.05$ and $0.06$ dex. Deep galaxies cannot fit this calibration themselves: if every mass is multiplied by $f$, then $g_{\rm bar}\to fg_{\rm bar}$ and $g_{\rm obs}^2=g_{\rm bar}a_0$ becomes $g_{\rm obs}^2=g_{\rm bar}(fa_0)$, so they measure only the product $fa_0$. The calibration must come from outside the rotation curves, in practice from gas masses measured galaxy by galaxy. The test is the mean of the $\Delta_i$ over the gated galaxies. Near 0 it supports a constant $a_0$; near $+0.22$ or above, an emergent or $H(z)$-tied scale; and if it fits none of them, it counts against all.

\section{Limits, and what is missing}
\emph{$\kappa$ is not derived:} it is fitted, and $0.461$ fits as well. \emph{A number is not a mechanism:} equation~(\ref{eq:relation}) fixes a value, not whether galaxies need modified gravity, modified inertia or dark matter, and it does not remove the need for the cold matter measured by the microwave background. If dark energy evolves \citep{DESI2025}, $a_0\propto\sqrt{\rho_{\rm DE}}$ would change by only about $-0.1$ dex at $z=2.5$. In short, $1/2$ fits SPARC but is not derived, and the missing input for a decision is gas mass measured galaxy by galaxy at $z\simeq2.5$.
