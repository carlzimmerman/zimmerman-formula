# Chapter 33: Standing on Shoulders: Who Found What

> *Before a road is built, surveyors walk the ground and drive brass benchmarks into the rock — small permanent markers that say: someone measured here first, and this is what they found. Every later map is built on those marks. This chapter walks the ground of this book and reads every benchmark by name.*

## Benchmarks in the Ground

If you have ever hiked a trail in old country, you may have come across one without knowing it: a small brass disk set flush into a boulder, stamped with a date and an elevation. It is called a survey benchmark. Some crew, decades ago, hauled instruments up that slope, measured the height of that exact spot against the level of the sea, and left the mark so that nobody would ever have to do it blind again. Every map of that mountain — every trail sign, every property line, every road grade — quietly rests on those disks. The people who set them are mostly forgotten. The marks are not. The marks are the point.

Science works the same way, and this chapter exists because, in the first edition of this book, I read some of the benchmarks wrong. Not the physics — the *names on the physics*. I described a prediction as "genuinely new, belonging to this framework and no other," and it was not new; two physicists and their student had driven that benchmark into the rock eighteen years earlier, and I had built on their mark without ever reading the stamp. This chapter is the correction, told in full, because a book that spends thirty-two chapters preaching both-ways honesty about *data* owes the same honesty about *credit*.

Where we are in the story: Parts 1 through 4 built the mystery and the two roads; Part 5 built the framework — the claim that the galactic acceleration scale a₀ is set by dark energy through one posited geometric factor; Part 6 walked the predictions, the tests, and the walls. What remains is the accounting that a referee, or a historian, would demand: for every load-bearing idea in this book, *who found it first?* You will see that the answer is almost never "the author," and I want to persuade you — genuinely, not as false modesty — that this is the *best* news in the whole book. Hold that thought; by the end of the chapter I hope you will see why.

## The Man Who Found the Scale

Every benchmark on this mountain sits downhill from one man.

In 1983, the Israeli physicist **Mordehai Milgrom** published three papers in the *Astrophysical Journal* (the first is ApJ 270, 365) proposing what he called MOND — Modified Newtonian Dynamics. We told this story in Chapter 17, but let me restate what he actually found, because the size of it is easy to lose.

Milgrom found *the scale*. Sifting through the galaxy data of his day, he noticed that the missing-mass problem does not switch on at a particular distance, or a particular mass, or a particular brightness. It switches on at a particular **acceleration** — one specific, absurdly gentle strength of gravitational pull, about 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared, roughly a hundred billion times weaker than the pull holding you to your chair. Above that threshold, Newton works and the books balance. Below it, every galaxy misbehaves, and misbehaves *the same way*. That number, **a₀**, is the single most important empirical discovery in this entire subject after the flat rotation curves themselves, and it is Milgrom's, full stop.

He found *the law*: below a₀, the effective acceleration follows the square-root form that makes rotation curves flatten and produces, as exact consequences, the regularities we met in Chapter 18 — the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation and, decades before it was measured, the shape of what is now called the radial-acceleration relation. When McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert plotted the RAR from 153 galaxies in 2016 (Phys. Rev. Lett. 117, 201101, arXiv:1609.05917) and the world briefly gasped, they were photographing a prediction Milgrom had written down when Return of the Jedi was in theaters.

And he found *the coincidence*. In those same 1983 papers, Milgrom remarked that his empirical a₀ was suspiciously close to the speed of light times the Hubble constant — a₀ ∼ cH₀ — a number built entirely out of *cosmology*, out of the universe's expansion, showing up uninvited in the spin of individual galaxies. After 1998, when the accelerating universe gave us the cosmological constant Λ, the coincidence sharpened: a₀ ∼ c²√Λ. Milgrom saw it, said it, and kept saying it for forty years — including, and this matters for what comes later in this chapter, the observation that *if* the coincidence is causal rather than accidental, a₀ might well vary over cosmic time as the cosmological quantities do. That remark was not a throwaway; he returned to the evolution question repeatedly, in print. The idea that "a₀ knows about the universe" is not this framework's idea. It is the framework's *inheritance*.

So let me set the first benchmark plainly. **Milgrom 1983: the scale a₀, the deep-MOND law, the Tully–Fisher and RAR regularities as predictions, and the a₀ ∼ cH₀ ∼ c²√Λ coincidence, evolution implications included.** Everything in Part 5 of this book stands on that disk.

## Inertia, Not Force — Milgrom Again, and the Physicists of Temperature

But the specific *kind* of theory this book bets on — modified **inertia**, the idea that it is the response of matter to force, not gravity itself, that changes at tiny accelerations — that must be the framework's own contribution, surely?

No. That is Milgrom's too, and the benchmark has a date: 1994.

In a paper in *Annals of Physics* (229, 384), Milgrom worked out what a modified-inertia formulation of MOND would have to look like — and proved a small, prophetic no-go theorem along the way: any such theory that respects Galilean invariance must be **nonlocal in time**, meaning a star's inertia cannot depend only on its acceleration *right now* but must know something about its whole trajectory. If you read Chapter 21 carefully, you will recognize that theorem as the seed of everything hard in this book's theoretical situation — the reason a covariant modified-inertia theory is not sitting on a shelf waiting to be used. Milgrom saw the wall from the doorway, in 1994, and posted a sign.

Then in 1999 he drove in the benchmark this framework most directly builds on (Phys. Lett. A 253, 273, astro-ph/9805346): the proposal that MOND's acceleration scale is a **vacuum effect** — that the inertia of an accelerating body is tied to how that body experiences the quantum vacuum, and that in a universe with a cosmological constant, the vacuum itself supplies exactly the right threshold. The de Sitter–Unruh mechanism at the heart of Chapter 21 — the idea I have spent a third of this book unpacking — is, in its essential physics, *Milgrom 1999*. When the first edition of this book occasionally read as though "acceleration, temperature, and inertia are secretly the same conversation" were a thought original to this framework, that was a benchmark misread, and I am correcting it here by name.

And Milgrom's 1999 paper itself stands on older marks, set by people who had no idea galaxies would ever be involved:

- **Fulling (1973), Davies (1975), and Unruh (1976)** discovered that acceleration has a temperature — that an accelerating observer in empty space feels a faint warm glow, the Unruh effect of Chapter 14.
- **Gibbons and Hawking (1977)** discovered that a universe with a cosmological constant has a temperature floor — the de Sitter horizon glow of Chapter 15.
- **Deser and Levin (1997)** (Class. Quantum Grav. 14, L163, gr-qc/9706018) computed the exact temperature felt by an observer accelerating *inside* a de Sitter universe — the beautiful Pythagorean formula combining both effects, which is the mathematical engine of this framework's interpolation.
- **Bekenstein (1973) and Hawking (1975)** taught us that horizons carry entropy, and **Cohen, Kaplan, and Nelson (1999)** (Phys. Rev. Lett. 82, 4971, hep-th/9803132) taught us how little energy a region of space can honestly hold — the CKN bound of Chapter 16, the language in which this framework's one free number is *stated*, though — as Chapter 23 proved — never *derived*.

> **Deeper Dive: The temperature lineage, in equations**
>
> The three benchmark temperatures, in one place. Unruh (1976): a detector with proper acceleration $a$ in flat spacetime sees thermal radiation at
> $$T_U = \frac{\hbar\, a}{2\pi c\, k_B}.$$
> Gibbons–Hawking (1977): an inertial observer in de Sitter space with cosmological constant $\Lambda$ sees the horizon glow at
> $$T_{dS} = \frac{\hbar\, c}{2\pi k_B}\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{3}}.$$
> Deser–Levin (1997): an observer with proper acceleration $a$ in de Sitter space sees the two combined *in quadrature* —
> $$T_{DL} = \frac{\hbar}{2\pi c\, k_B}\sqrt{a^2 + \frac{c^4\Lambda}{3}},$$
> the "hypotenuse temperature." Milgrom (1999) proposed reading the *excess* of this temperature over the de Sitter floor, $\Delta T = T_{DL} - T_{dS}$, as the driver of inertia: at large $a$ the excess is linear in $a$ (Newton recovered); at small $a$ it is quadratic in $a$ (the deep-MOND regime), with the crossover set by $a_\Lambda = c^2\sqrt{\Lambda/3}$ — cosmology's acceleration, appearing in the inertia of a star. Every equation in this box predates this framework; the framework's contribution to *this box* is zero, and saying so precisely is the point of the chapter. Concurrently with this book's second edition, Luo (2026, arXiv:2602.14515) has developed the same dS–Unruh mechanism independently, with a static a₀ — a point we will return to below, because independent arrivals are themselves data.

## The Paper I Should Have Found in 2008: Limbach, Psaltis, and Özel

Now I have to tell you the story this chapter was really written for, and I am going to tell it the way it happened, because the manner of the mistake is as instructive as the mistake.

Through the first edition of this book, and in talks, and in papers, I described one prediction as the framework's crown: *if a₀ is set by dark energy, and dark energy evolves, then a₀ must evolve too* — a₀(z) ∝ √ρ_DE(z) — *and you can test this by looking at the Tully–Fisher relation in the deep past.* I called it "the framework's one genuinely new, parameter-free prediction." I called it "the prediction that belongs to this framework and no other." I believed both sentences when I wrote them. I had searched the literature — not hard enough — and found nothing.

In June of 2026, during the systematic audit that produced this second edition, the search was finally done properly. And there it was: a paper posted to the arXiv on the 17th of September, 2008 — **Limbach, Psaltis & Özel, arXiv:0809.2790** — *proposing the redshift evolution of the Tully–Fisher relation as a test of modified gravity.* Dimitrios Psaltis and Feryal Özel were professors at the University of Arizona, best known for their work on neutron stars and, later, for their central roles in the Event Horizon Telescope's photograph of a black hole. Marissa Limbach was their student. In 2008 — before DESI existed, before "evolving dark energy" was a headline, when I had never written a line about any of this — they sat down and reasoned: *if* Milgrom's coincidence is causal, *if* a₀ is really set by the dark-energy density, then a₀ must track that density through cosmic time. Their Equation 4 ties the acceleration scale to the dark-energy density — the very tracking law, a₀(z) ∝ √ρ_DE(z), that I spent Chapter 25 presenting. And they then built the very test: the zero point of the Tully–Fisher relation, measured at high redshift, as the observable that rises or falls with a₀. The tracking law *and* its test. Both benchmarks. 2008.

I want to be honest about what finding that paper felt like, because you may someday feel it about work of your own, and it helps to know the sequence. The first feeling is a cold drop in the stomach — eighteen years, and I never looked hard enough. The second feeling, arriving perhaps a minute later, is embarrassment with a very specific shape: this book's own Chapter 3 tells the story of Fritz Zwicky, the man who found the missing mass in 1933 and was overlooked for forty years, and I wrote that chapter as a warning about how the community treats benchmarks it finds inconvenient — and then I walked past one myself. Not out of malice; out of insufficient searching, which history does not distinguish as carefully as we'd like.

But the third feeling is the one I want you to sit with, because it is the true one, and it took me longest to reach: **relief, and then something close to joy.** Here is why. Read that sentence again, because it is the hinge of the chapter: *if an idea is found once, it might be a quirk of one mind; if it is found independently, decades apart, by people with different training, different tools, and different motivations, the thing they keep finding is probably in the world and not in their heads.* Psaltis and Özel were not MOND partisans; they came from neutron-star astrophysics, from the hard-headed end of the field. They looked at the same coincidence Milgrom saw in 1983 — the one this framework is built on — and it struck *them* as worth testing too. And in 2026, Luo (arXiv:2602.14515) arrived at the dS–Unruh inertia mechanism yet again, independently, concurrent with this book. Three separate arrivals at the same benchmark is not a threat to the idea. It is the strongest evidence I can offer you that the coincidence between a₀ and the vacuum is *real enough to keep being discovered*.

So the correction stands, in this edition, everywhere: the a₀ ∝ √ρ_DE tracking law and its Tully–Fisher redshift test are due to **Limbach, Psaltis & Özel (2008)**, with Milgrom's evolution remarks as their ancestor. They got there first. The ledger now says so.

> **Deeper Dive: The LPO test, made quantitative**
>
> The deep-MOND/baryonic Tully–Fisher relation ties a galaxy's flat rotation speed to its baryonic mass through a₀:
> $$v_f^4 = G\, M_b\, a_0.$$
> Take the tracking law as LPO's Equation 4 gives it, $a_0(z) = a_0(0)\sqrt{\rho_{\rm DE}(z)/\rho_{\rm DE}(0)}$, and the observable follows in one line: at fixed baryonic mass,
> $$\frac{v_f(z)}{v_f(0)} = \left(\frac{a_0(z)}{a_0(0)}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{\rho_{\rm DE}(z)}{\rho_{\rm DE}(0)}\right)^{1/8}.$$
> The eighth root is brutal — it is why Chapter 26 insists on a *sign-first* test program: the magnitude of any offset is softened twice over, but its *sign* survives every softening. For a true cosmological constant, ρ_DE is constant, the ratio is exactly 1 at every redshift, and the test returns null — which is why the whole program is hostage to DESI (Chapter 25). What this framework added to LPO's proposal, and all it added: the specific 32π normalization of a₀ (a choice, Chapter 20), and the observation that DESI's *phantom-crossing* dark energy makes ρ_DE(z) non-monotonic, so a₀(z) should show a small **bump** — a rise of +3–9% near z ≈ 0.4 before the long decline (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21110936). The bump shape is, as far as I can determine — and I have now learned to search properly — the only bump-shaped a₀(z) prediction in the field. It is also below current detection floors, and dead if w = −1. Both facts in the same breath, as always.

## The Neighbors: Verlinde and McCulloch

Once you start reading benchmarks honestly, you also have to read the ones belonging to ideas that *compete* with yours, and read them with the same fairness this book gave ΛCDM.

**Erik Verlinde** (2011, JHEP 04, 029, arXiv:1001.0785; and 2016, SciPost Phys. 2, 016, arXiv:1611.02269) built *emergent gravity*: the proposal that gravity is not fundamental at all but a thermodynamic bookkeeping of information on horizons — and in his 2016 paper, the specific claim that the dark-energy content of de Sitter space produces exactly a MOND-like extra pull in galaxies, with the scale set by cH₀. Notice what that is: another independent arrival, by a prominent string theorist, at *the same coincidence* — the vacuum setting the galactic scale. Verlinde's specific construction makes its own predictions, some of which have fared well in lensing confrontations and some of which have not; it is a rival, and this book does not adopt it. But the benchmark is his, and the neighborhood is shared.

**Mike McCulloch** (from 2007, MNRAS 376, 338, onward) built *quantized inertia*: the proposal that inertia itself comes from Unruh radiation made inhomogeneous by horizons, weakening at accelerations near cH₀. His framework is further from the mainstream than anything else in this chapter, its detailed confrontations with data are contested, and I want to be plain that most physicists do not accept it. But the honest ledger requires the entry: McCulloch was arguing that *inertia, Unruh temperature, and the cosmic horizon are one subject* — the thesis of this book's Part 3 — years before I wrote a word of it, from his own direction, with his own machinery. When several independent prospectors keep striking the same vein from different slopes, you may doubt each prospector's map. You should start believing in the vein.

## Those Who Built the Hard Parts

Four more benchmarks, each carrying a piece of this book that I could never have supplied.

**Skordis and Złośnik (2021)** (Phys. Rev. Lett. 127, 161302, arXiv:2007.00082) built AeST — the first fully relativistic theory in the MOND family that survives the modern gauntlet: gravitational waves at the speed of light, a working cosmic microwave background fit, MOND behavior in galaxies. Every time this book has said "the framework's only *written* relativistic realization," it has meant their theory, and the debt is enormous. And I must state the debt with both hands: AeST achieves its CMB fit by including a dust-like component — a **ghost condensate**, a fluid made of the vacuum's own settling, like dew condensing out of air that looked empty — and it was *the authors themselves* who identified their dark sector as such. Chapters 28 and 29 leaned on that identification for the honest cluster accounting: the residual is real, the condensate carries it, and its amount is a free input. Their theory; their candor; our inheritance.

**Milgrom, once more** — because the machinery this book uses to even *pose* the question "is nature modified inertia or modified gravity?" is also his, developed across a line of papers from 2011 to 2022 (arXiv:1111.1611; MNRAS 437, 2531, arXiv:1212.2568; Phys. Rev. D 106, 064060, arXiv:2201.01772). The clean statements of what modified-inertia theories must predict differently — in the Solar System, in external-field effects, in high-frequency orbits — are Milgrom's discriminants. The first edition of this book sometimes presented that machinery as though the framework had sharpened it; the machinery was sharp when I found it.

**Desmond, Hees, and Famaey (2024)** (MNRAS 530, 1781), with the follow-up of Park and collaborators (2026, arXiv:2602.17884), computed the Cassini **quadrupole** bound — the Q₂ distortion of Saturn's orbit that the galactic external field should induce in any modified-gravity MOND theory. Their result is the reason the second edition of this book had to retract its Solar-System "win": the γ-pass every MOND theory shares turned out to be trivial, and the sharp in-hand test, theirs, currently reads as a 3–15σ *tension* that the framework's written realization inherits (Chapter 26). It is a strange kind of gratitude one owes the authors of one's own bad news, but it is real gratitude: they built the knife. That the knife currently sits against this framework's throat does not change whose knife it is.

**McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert** — the SPARC database (AJ 152, 157, arXiv:1606.09251) and the radial-acceleration relation (Phys. Rev. Lett. 117, 201101). Essentially every galaxy-scale number in this book is measured against their patient, decade-long accounting of 175 rotation curves. Data first; everyone else second.

## What, Then, Is Actually Ours

So after the full survey — after Milgrom's scale, law, coincidence, mechanism, and discriminants; after Limbach, Psaltis & Özel's tracking law and test; after Deser–Levin's temperature, Skordis–Złośnik's realization, Verlinde's and McCulloch's parallel arrivals, and Desmond–Hees–Famaey's bound — what is left? What did *this* framework, this book, this author, actually add?

Here is the complete list. I have checked it against the audit twice, and I state it exactly and not a hair more.

**Four claims:**

1. **The 32π normalization** — the specific coefficient in a₀ = c²√(Λ/32π), equivalently the posited κ = ½ inside Z = √(32π/3). A *choice*, not a derivation — Chapter 23 proved no consistency requirement forces it — but a specific, committed choice that makes every prediction in this book numerically sharp rather than order-of-magnitude.
2. **The non-monotonic a₀(z) bump** — the +3–9% rise at z ≈ 0.4 that follows from marrying LPO's tracking law to DESI's phantom-crossing dark energy (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21110936). Below current floors; dead if w = −1; and, so far as an honest search can determine, the only bump-shaped a₀(z) prediction in the field.
3. **The cluster η(β) anisotropy sign** — the prediction that a cluster's apparent mass residual slides with the orbital anisotropy of its galaxies in a direction modified gravity cannot produce (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21104820). Underpowered today; MG-impossible in principle; Chapter 29's crown jewel.
4. **The s^TX target** — the specific, computable Lorentz-violation coefficient that the framework's preferred frame *induces* (never derives) in the Standard-Model-Extension bookkeeping, with the boost dipole toward the cosmic microwave background's apex as the live observable (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20978308; Chapter 30).

**And four theorems — doors this project proved shut:**

- The **κ-closure theorem** (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20738055): κ = ½ is provably unforceable; every consistency requirement leaves it free. The framework's one knob is a knob by *theorem*, not by fatigue.
- The **cluster no-go** (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20779562): pure modified inertia provably cannot close the cluster residual; the full non-local kernel floors at the mean-field value, and anisotropy makes it worse. The residual *requires* matter.
- The **covariant-MI trichotomy** and the **number-field theorem** (*Scale Without Law*, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21016309): every route to a covariant modified-inertia theory is blocked (see the Deeper Dive below), and no horizon-thermodynamic normalization can ever output Z, because Z lives in the wrong family of numbers.

Notice the shape of that ledger. One choice, one shape, one sign, one target — and four proofs that doors are *shut*. If you came to this chapter expecting the author's column to be the longest, I understand; the first edition sometimes wrote as if it were. It is the shortest. And I have come to believe that the no-go theorems are the most valuable entries in it — because in a field crowded with hopeful construction, a proof that a door is shut saves every future builder the decade they would have spent walking into it. Proving a door shut is also a contribution. It is mostly the kind this project has made, and this edition says so without flinching.

> **Worked Example: Reading the coincidence for yourself — and seeing exactly where the choice enters**
>
> Let us do the arithmetic that everyone in this chapter, from Milgrom to LPO to Verlinde, has done in some form — slowly, so you can see precisely which part is nature's and which part is a decision.
>
> *Given:* the measured cosmological constant, Λ ≈ 1.10 × 10⁻⁵² m⁻², and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.
>
> **Step 1 — cosmology's own acceleration.** The de Sitter expansion rate is $H_\Lambda = c\sqrt{\Lambda/3}$:
> $$H_\Lambda = 3.00\times10^{8}\times\sqrt{3.67\times10^{-53}} \approx 3.00\times10^{8}\times 6.06\times10^{-27} \approx 1.82\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.$$
> Multiply by c to make an acceleration:
> $$c\,H_\Lambda \approx (3.00\times10^{8})(1.82\times10^{-18}) \approx 5.45\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$
>
> **Step 2 — compare to the galaxies.** Milgrom's empirical scale is a₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s². The ratio:
> $$\frac{c\,H_\Lambda}{a_0} \approx \frac{5.45\times10^{-10}}{1.2\times10^{-10}} \approx 4.5.$$
> A pure number of order a few. *That* is the coincidence — the 1983 benchmark. Nature supplies it; nobody chose it.
>
> **Step 3 — the framework's choice.** This framework posits the divisor Z = √(32π/3) ≈ 5.789 — equivalently κ = ½ — giving
> $$a_0 = \frac{c\,H_\Lambda}{Z} \approx \frac{5.45\times10^{-10}}{5.789} \approx 9.4\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m/s^2},$$
> the canonical 9.36 × 10⁻¹¹ of Chapter 20. Look hard at the two steps: nature's ratio is "about 4.5"; the framework *declares* it to be 5.789. The declaration sits comfortably inside what the galaxy data permit (Chapter 18 showed the data cannot distinguish 9.36 from 12 in these units), but it is a declaration. Milgrom's benchmark is Step 2. The framework's entire numerical claim is Step 3 — and the κ-closure theorem says Step 3 can never be promoted to a derivation. One coincidence found by others; one number chosen here. That is the whole division of credit, in arithmetic.

> **Deeper Dive: The trichotomy — why the mechanism still has no covariant home**
>
> *Scale Without Law* (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21016309) proves that the three exhaustive routes to a covariant modified-inertia completion are individually blocked:
>
> 1. **Local route** — make inertia depend on higher time-derivatives of the trajectory: blocked by the Ostrogradsky instability (a ghost — states of unboundedly negative energy — the generic fatal disease of higher-derivative theories).
> 2. **Field route** — mediate the modification through a new field: the theory becomes modified *gravity* in all but name, and inherits the Solar-System constraints — the Cassini quadrupole band — that motivated the inertia route in the first place.
> 3. **Nonlocal route** — let inertia depend on the trajectory's history through the vacuum bath (Milgrom's 1994 nonlocality, taken seriously): the from-the-bath kernel provably produces the *wrong sign* — anti-MOND — an enhancement of inertia at low accelerations rather than the observed deficit.
>
> The same paper proves the positive results in the same breath: the dS–Unruh temperature *forces* the scale a₀ ∼ c²√Λ, the deep-MOND law, and the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation (Milgrom's 1999 mechanism made exact) — while forcing *neither* the interpolation (which collapses to one unforced coefficient), *nor* Z (the number-field theorem: thermodynamic normalizations live in ℚ(π), Z in ℚ(√π), so no entropy factor can output it), *nor* a covariant equation of motion (this trichotomy). A subsequent exhaustive hunt for an escape from the third horn's sign theorem (`real_research/PUMP_HUNT_AND_TRIGGERS_2026-07.md`) killed every candidate — including AeST's own μ*c mode, whose response flips sign at radii of 1.7–5.2 kpc and is thereby falsified by the one-signed RAR — leaving a precise, unoccupied specification: open, but empty. A would-be builder now has a spec sheet, not a blank page. That spec sheet is a benchmark too, and it is one of ours.

## The Zwicky Lesson, Pointed at Myself

Chapter 3 told you about Fritz Zwicky: right in 1933, difficult in person, overlooked for forty years, vindicated not by his boldness but by independent measurement. I asked you then to keep the story in your pocket for when we reached the controversial parts. This is the pocket, and the story comes out pointed at me.

The Zwicky lesson is usually told as a warning to the *community*: do not dismiss the uncomfortable outsider. But it has a second edge, quieter and just as sharp, aimed at the outsider himself: *being first matters; crediting first matters more.* Zwicky was wronged by a community that did not read his benchmark. Limbach, Psaltis, and Özel were wronged — mildly, unintentionally, but truly — by an author who did not search hard enough for theirs. The two failures are the same failure at different scales. A field that loses track of who found what will misjudge *every* new claim, because priority is how a field audits itself: it is the record of which ideas arose independently (evidence they are real), which arose from each other (evidence of a lineage), and which arose from nothing (evidence of a mistake).

In 2026 I also had to make a harder correction than a citation. In June of that year I publicly retracted, to the roughly forty physicists I had been writing to, a set of overclaims — that this framework was on a road to a theory of everything, that it reached into the Standard Model. It was not, and it does not, and Chapter 31 tells that story in full. I bring it up here for one reason: the retraction and this chapter are the same discipline applied to different ledgers. One says: *do not claim more than the evidence supports.* The other says: *do not claim more than you actually did.* A theory can survive a failed prediction — that is what Chapter 34's scoreboard is for. What no theory, and no author, survives is a corrupted ledger, because then even the true entries stop being believed.

So here is the moral of the chapter, stated as plainly as I can: **priority is a ledger, not a trophy.** A trophy sits on one shelf and gathers dust. A ledger is a working document — it tells you which coincidences have been found often enough to trust, which walls have been hit often enough to route around, and exactly how tall you stand only because of who is underneath. Milgrom found the scale and the mechanism. Limbach, Psaltis, and Özel found the tracking law and its test. Deser and Levin found the temperature. Skordis and Złośnik built the realization. Desmond, Hees, and Famaey built the sharpest knife. Verlinde, McCulloch, and Luo keep finding the same vein from other slopes. What this framework added is one choice, one bump, one sign, one target, and four locked doors — a real contribution, honestly scoped, and small against the mountain it stands on. It is not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be. But its ledger, as of this edition, is clean — and a clean ledger is the one thing in this book I can promise you outright, because it is the only thing here that depends on honesty rather than on nature.

Nature's turn comes next, in the final chapter, where every promise in this book is converted into a number, a date, and a way to die.

## Summary

- Science rests on **benchmarks** — priority marks that record who measured what first. This chapter reads every benchmark under this framework by name, because the first edition misread the most important one.
- **Milgrom** owns the deepest marks: the acceleration scale a₀, the deep-MOND law and its Tully–Fisher/RAR consequences (1983), the modified-*inertia* formulation and its nonlocality no-go (1994), the de Sitter–vacuum mechanism this book builds on (1999), and the modified-inertia-vs-modified-gravity discriminant machinery (2011–2022). The temperature physics beneath it belongs to Fulling, Davies, Unruh, Gibbons–Hawking, and **Deser–Levin** (1997).
- **Limbach, Psaltis & Özel (2008, arXiv:0809.2790)** proposed the a₀(z) ∝ √ρ_DE(z) tracking law (their Eq. 4) *and* its Tully–Fisher redshift test — eighteen years before the first edition called that prediction "genuinely new."
