# Could Dark Energy Set the Scale of Galaxies?

*An independent researcher argues that the strange acceleration scale governing galaxy rotation is set by the same energy speeding up cosmic expansion. The idea is economical, brave, and — crucially — about to face its decisive tests.*

---

Spin a galaxy and something does not add up. Out at the edges of a spiral like our own Milky Way, the stars and gas clouds orbit far faster than the visible matter can explain. By the ordinary rules of gravity, those outer stars should be flung loose, like riders thrown from a merry-go-round spun too fast. They are not. For half a century, the leading explanation has been that each galaxy sits inside a vast, invisible halo of "dark matter" — some unknown particle, never directly detected, that supplies the missing gravitational glue. The hunt for that particle has consumed billions of dollars and many careers, and it remains, for now, empty-handed.

There has long been a stubborn alternative. In the early 1980s the physicist Mordehai Milgrom noticed that the discrepancy does not switch on at a particular distance or a particular mass, but at a particular *acceleration*. Wherever the gravitational tug on a star falls below a specific, universal threshold — about one ten-billionth of the acceleration you feel standing on Earth — the rules appear to change and the missing-mass effect appears. Milgrom proposed modifying the law of gravity itself below that threshold, an approach known as MOND, for Modified Newtonian Dynamics. MOND fits the rotation of hundreds of galaxies with almost eerie precision and a single new constant of nature, written *a-naught*. But it has never explained where that constant comes from, and it has well-documented troubles in galaxy clusters and with gravitational lensing. Most cosmologists regard it as a curiosity at best, dark matter's stubborn younger sibling.

Now an independent researcher named Carl P. Zimmerman — who runs a small software company, Briar Creek Tech, and pursues physics on his own time — has put forward a framework that tries to answer the question MOND never could: not *whether* there is a special acceleration, but *why it has the value it does*. His answer is that the number is written into the sky already, in the energy that is pushing the universe apart.

## A coincidence fifty years old

Begin with two numbers that, on the face of it, have nothing to do with each other.

The first is Milgrom's acceleration, *a-naught*, the threshold below which galaxies misbehave: roughly 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared. It is a fact about the insides of galaxies, measured from how stars orbit.

The second comes from the largest scale we know. In 1998 astronomers discovered that the expansion of the universe is not slowing under gravity, as everyone expected, but speeding up, driven by a mysterious "dark energy" that behaves like Einstein's old cosmological constant, written with the Greek letter Lambda. Dark energy has its own characteristic acceleration — the gentle outward push it exerts on the cosmos. And that cosmic acceleration scale turns out to be almost exactly the same as Milgrom's *a-naught*.

This is not a new observation; physicists have remarked on the coincidence for decades and mostly filed it under "interesting." Why should the threshold governing the outskirts of a single galaxy have anything to do with the energy budget of the entire cosmos? The two phenomena live on scales separated by a factor of millions. The standard assumption has been that the resemblance is a numerical accident, a near-miss that means nothing.

Zimmerman's framework — he calls it de Sitter-MOND, after the Dutch astronomer Willem de Sitter, whose name attaches to a universe filled with dark energy — is built on the conviction that the coincidence is no accident at all. It is, he argues, cause and effect. Dark energy *sets* the galactic threshold. Write it as a clean relationship and the two numbers lock together: the galaxy scale equals the speed of light times a factor built from Lambda, and the prediction lands at about 9.4 × 10⁻¹¹ meters per second squared — within shouting distance of the measured value. The structure of that formula contains no fitted factor; the single geometric choice that fixes its overall value comes a moment later, and is worth being careful about.

## Where the resistance comes from

A formula that ties two numbers together is suggestive, but it is not yet physics. The harder question is *mechanism*: by what physical process could the energy of empty space reach into a galaxy and change how its stars move? Here the framework leans on a genuinely strange and genuinely real piece of physics.

One of the deep lessons of the last half-century is that empty space is not truly empty, and that what counts as "empty" depends on how you are moving. An accelerating observer in a vacuum is bathed in a faint warmth that an observer drifting at constant speed does not feel — the Unruh effect, a cousin of the radiation that leaks from black holes. In a universe with dark energy, there is a further twist: such a universe has an irreducible temperature floor, a faint thermal hum that pervades all of space and cannot be cooled away. It is fantastically cold — far colder than the deepest intergalactic void — but it is not zero.

Zimmerman's proposal, building on a path Milgrom himself opened in the 1990s and on theoretical work by Stanley Deser and Janna Levin, is that this temperature floor changes how hard it is to push on things. Inertia — an object's resistance to being accelerated, the property that makes a boulder harder to shove than a pebble — is in this picture not a fixed, God-given quantity but something that emerges from an object's relationship with the vacuum around it. When a star out in a galaxy's lonely outskirts is barely accelerating at all, drifting in near-free-fall, it begins to "feel" that cosmic temperature floor, and that interaction shows up as *extra resistance to being pushed*. Extra inertia. From the outside, a star that is harder to accelerate looks exactly like a star feeling extra gravity — like a star embedded in dark matter.

This is the part worth slowing down on, because it is where the framework earns its distinctive predictions. It modifies *inertia*, not the force of gravity. The two sound interchangeable but are not. Because the effect depends on how feebly an object is accelerating, it simply switches off wherever accelerations are high — inside the Solar System, say, where the planets are firmly in gravity's grip. That is not a patch added to dodge a problem; it falls out of the construction. It is also why the framework sails past one of the sharpest tests in physics: the Cassini spacecraft's exquisitely precise tracking of Saturn's orbit, which has ruled out any extra force at that level. A modified-inertia theory predicts no such force there, and so survives by design.

## One knob, not six

If the idea stopped at a suggestive formula and a colorful mechanism, it would be one more entry in the crowded catalog of dark-matter alternatives. What its author argues sets it apart is parsimony, and he has tried to make that claim precise.

The reigning model of cosmology, called Lambda-CDM, is a genuine triumph — it accounts for the afterglow of the Big Bang, the growth of cosmic structure, and much else, with remarkable economy. But its economy still runs to six independently adjusted numbers, plus the assumption of a dark-matter particle that, decades into the search, no experiment has caught.

The de Sitter-MOND framework, Zimmerman argues, runs on essentially one free choice — a single geometric factor, a one-half, that sets the strength of the coupling. Much of his recent work is an attempt to show that this lone factor resists being pinned down by the usual deeper principles — the requirements of a healthy quantum theory, holography, a count of nature's known fields — and is instead pure geometry: the ratio between the way mass curves space and the way a sphere fills volume. Whether or not every physicist accepts that accounting, the ambition is clear. One knob, fixed by geometry, versus six numbers and an undetected particle.

It is essential to be honest about what this does *not* mean. The framework does not derive the value of *a-naught* from nothing; that one-half is a posit, an input chosen for geometric reasons, not a result handed down by the theory. So this is a one-parameter theory, not a parameter-free one. Zimmerman is candid on the point, and the distinction matters: a theory that *predicted* the special number from pure logic would be a different and far stronger animal.

## The test that could settle it

Here is what lifts the proposal above philosophy, and what makes it worth a careful reader's attention: it makes a sharp, falsifiable prediction that the world is now in a position to check.

If the galactic acceleration scale really is set by dark energy, then it should not be a true constant. It should *track dark energy across cosmic time*. Run the clock backward to the early universe, when the dark-energy density was different, and the threshold *a-naught* should have been correspondingly different. Galaxies in the deep past should reveal a measurably different number than galaxies today.

This is where a live controversy in cosmology becomes the framework's proving ground. For years dark energy was assumed to be a perfect cosmological constant — the same everywhere, for all time. But recent results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), a survey mapping millions of galaxies, hint that dark energy may instead be *evolving* — slightly stronger in the past, weakening now. If that signal holds, Zimmerman's framework makes a specific and peculiar prediction: the galactic threshold should trace a non-monotonic curve through cosmic history, rising by a few percent around redshift 0.4 (a moderately distant epoch) before falling to roughly three-quarters of its present value by redshift 3, deep in the young universe.

The cleanest place to look is in the rotation of distant galaxies. There is a tight relationship, the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation, between how fast a galaxy spins and how much ordinary matter it contains — and the special acceleration sets exactly where that relation kicks in. If *a-naught* was smaller in the early universe, then galaxies at redshift 3 should spin about 7 percent slow relative to the local rule. That is a small effect, but it is the kind of thing the next generation of instruments is built to measure. DESI's future data releases, around 2026 and 2027, together with high-redshift galaxy kinematics from the Extremely Large Telescope, the James Webb Space Telescope, and the ALMA radio array over the years that follow, will put a real number on the question. The prediction is sharp enough to be wrong.

There is a second, entirely different line of attack. A theory that singles out a cosmic rest frame — and this one does, because it pegs local physics to the universe's dark-energy bath — is technically a violation of Einstein's principle that all uniformly moving observers are equivalent. That makes the framework a member of a well-studied class of "Standard Model Extension" theories, and it implies a tiny, computable directional signal in precision laboratory and Solar-System experiments: a Lorentz-violation that present technology can already constrain and may soon detect, along with a falsifiable claim that the violation should preserve a particular symmetry while breaking another. It is a second, independent door through which the idea can be tested — and falsified.

## The honest ledger

A responsible account has to set the appeal beside the liabilities, and they are real.

First and most important: this is not a theory of everything, and Zimmerman does not pretend otherwise. It is a theory of gravity and the dark sector — full stop, in his own framing, "not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be." It says nothing about the particles of the Standard Model. It does not explain the mass of the proton, the pattern of the lepton masses, or the forces of the subatomic world; all of that is walled off, exactly as it is for every other current theory of gravity.

Second, gravitational lensing — the bending of light by mass, one of the most powerful tools astronomers have — is a genuine weak point. Part of Zimmerman's own work is a no-go theorem showing that a fully consistent, Solar-System-safe version of MOND-style lensing cannot be built within the obvious rules. The upshot is that the framework's account of how it bends light remains an unfinished, hand-fitted piece — the same unfinished business that the leading relativistic version of MOND, a theory called AeST, also carries. It is an honest gap, not a solved problem.

Third, the galaxy relationships the framework fits so well — the rotation curves, the Tully-Fisher relation — are *shared with the entire MOND family*. They are not unique to this idea, and fitting them is not, by itself, evidence for this version over any other. The genuinely distinctive content is narrower: the evolving *a-naught* and the Solar-System Lorentz-violation signal. Everything else is held in common with a body of work that the mainstream has already weighed and largely set aside.

Fourth, and sharpest: the marquee prediction is a hostage to a contested result. The evolving-dark-energy signal from DESI is exciting but not settled; several reanalyses find that its strength depends heavily on choices made in the data modeling, and under some of those choices the evidence softens considerably. If dark energy turns out to be a plain, unchanging cosmological constant after all, then the framework's signature prediction flattens out and its distinctive content dissolves back into ordinary MOND — with all of MOND's unsolved problems in clusters and lensing intact. The framework has, in effect, bet its uniqueness on a cosmological measurement it does not control.

And the largest caveat frames all the others: this is the work of a single independent researcher, published openly but not yet through the slow grind of peer review and replication that turns a proposal into established science. The overwhelming weight of professional cosmology still favors particle dark matter and the standard model of the universe, for good reasons rooted in a vast body of evidence — the cosmic microwave background and the large-scale structure of the cosmos chief among them — that MOND-family theories have historically struggled to match. None of what follows should be read as a verdict against that consensus.

## Why it is worth watching anyway

So why give the idea a hearing? Because it does the thing good science is supposed to do. It takes a coincidence that the field has shrugged at for fifty years and refuses to shrug. It offers a concrete mechanism, drawn from real and accepted physics, rather than a free-floating fit. It is economical, trading six parameters and a missing particle for a single geometric choice. And — most importantly — it does not hide behind untestability. It stakes itself on specific numbers, in specific experiments, on a timetable measured in years rather than generations.

That is the valuable shape of the thing. Not "scientist solves dark matter" — nothing here is solved, and the author is the first to say so. Rather: here is a spare, falsifiable proposal that ties the smallest puzzle of galaxies to the largest puzzle of the cosmos, and that has been explicit about exactly how it could be proven wrong. Over the next several years, the telescopes and the laboratories will answer. Either the special number of galaxies will be caught drifting in step with the dark energy of the universe, or it will sit stubbornly still and the idea will quietly close. That willingness to be tested is, in the end, what makes it worth the watching.
