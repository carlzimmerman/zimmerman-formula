# Is the cosmological constant the source of MOND's acceleration scale? An independent proposal faces its tests

*An independent researcher argues that the acceleration below which galaxy rotation curves go anomalous is fixed by dark energy. The idea is economical and falsifiable — but it inherits MOND's old wounds at clusters and in lensing, and its sharpest prediction now hangs on a contested cosmological signal.*

---

For half a century, a single number has shadowed the study of galaxies. Below an acceleration of roughly 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ metres per second squared — about a hundred-billionth of Earth's surface gravity — the orbital speeds of stars and gas in galaxy outskirts stop falling the way Newton and the visible mass say they should. Rotation curves flatten. The standard resolution is dark matter: unseen particles whose gravity props up the curves, embedded in the broader success of the ΛCDM model. The older, more austere alternative is Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), Mordehai Milgrom's 1983 proposal that dynamics itself departs from the Newtonian prediction below a universal acceleration scale, conventionally written *a₀*.

That MOND scale has long carried a tantalizing numerical accident. The acceleration one builds from the cosmological constant, *cH₀* or equivalently *c*²√Λ, lands within a factor of order unity of *a₀*. Milgrom himself flagged the coincidence decades ago, and it has been a recurring item on the field's list of "facts a complete theory ought to explain." Most cosmologists file it under suggestive-but-unexplained, alongside the comparable magnitudes of the dark-matter and dark-energy densities today — a numerical near-equality that may be deep or may be nothing.

Carl P. Zimmerman, an independent researcher who runs a software company in North Carolina and works on physics outside any academic appointment, has spent the past stretch trying to convert that coincidence into a causal claim. In three papers posted openly to the Zenodo repository in 2026, he sets out a framework he calls de Sitter–MOND. Its one-line proposition: *a₀* is not a free constant of nature but is *set by dark energy*, through the relation

*a₀* = *c*²√(Λ/32π) = (*c*/2)√(*G*ρ_DE) = *cH*_Λ /*Z*,

with *Z* = √(32π/3) ≈ 5.789. Insert the measured dark-energy density and the framework returns *a₀* ≈ 9.36 × 10⁻¹¹ m s⁻², a hair below the canonical MOND value and comfortably inside the scatter of values fit to real galaxies.

The proposal is bold, narrow, and — its author is at pains to insist — incomplete. "It is not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be," Zimmerman writes. What follows is an attempt to weigh, for a reader fluent in general relativity and cosmology, what the framework genuinely buys and what it conspicuously does not.

## A temperature floor as the engine

The physical mechanism Zimmerman invokes is not a new force but modified *inertia*, and it leans on respectable antecedents: Milgrom's 1999 reading of MOND as a vacuum effect, and the Deser–Levin analysis of Unruh-type radiation seen by accelerated observers in de Sitter space. The logic runs as follows. A universe with a positive cosmological constant has a de Sitter horizon, and that horizon endows the vacuum with an irreducible temperature floor — a de Sitter–Unruh temperature that does not switch off. An object in genuine free fall, drifting in a galaxy's outskirts where accelerations are tiny, samples that floor. The claim is that this background manifests as extra resistance to being accelerated — extra inertia — which, viewed from outside, masquerades as extra gravity.

The architecture has an attractive built-in feature. Because the effect modifies inertia rather than adding a fifth force, it is naturally negligible where accelerations are large. In the Solar System the margin is wide: at Saturn's orbit, the relevant locus for the tightest planetary test, the local acceleration sits some five to six orders of magnitude above *a₀* (in the framework's own bookkeeping, *a/a₀* ≈ 6.9 × 10⁵), and the correction is suppressed by construction. This is the standard MOND escape from Solar-System tests, here grounded in a specific vacuum-thermodynamic story rather than asserted through an interpolating function.

That suppression is not merely qualitative, and it yields the framework's strongest card actually in hand. Evaluated on the modified-inertia gate, the fractional departure from Newtonian behaviour at Saturn is about 7 × 10⁻⁷ — a factor of roughly 30 below the Cassini bound on any anomalous precession, |γ − 1| < 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ (Bertotti, Iess & Tortora 2003). So the framework passes Cassini comfortably. The point worth dwelling on is that this margin is *diagnostic*: an *ungated* modified-*gravity* completion of the same *a₀* — the kind of relativistic-MOND construction in which the modification is a genuine extra force rather than an inertial effect — would instead produce a Solar-System deviation of order √(*a₀*/*g*) ∼ 10⁻³, and would *fail* the same bound by orders of magnitude. Cassini, already analysed, therefore discriminates: it favours the modified-inertia reading and excludes a modified-gravity host. In a research program whose distinctive content is mostly years from test, this is the one sharp, in-hand result, and it cuts in the framework's favour — though only for the inertial version of the idea.

Two things about the *form* of the result deserve to be stated precisely, because they are the framework's other strong cards. First, the dependence *a₀* ∼ *c*²√Λ is, in Zimmerman's analysis, over-determined: a self-audited count finds four structurally independent lines of argument forcing the same square-root-of-Λ scaling, so the functional form is argued to be not a lucky guess but a robust feature of dimensional and geometric reasoning about a de Sitter background. Second, the numerical kernel √(8π/3) — including its half-integer power of π — is, on the author's decomposition, fixed by familiar constants: the 8π of the Einstein field equations and the factor of 3 in the Friedmann equation, with the entire residual freedom collected into a single dimensionless coefficient. The shape of the relation, in other words, is argued to be comparatively rigid; the count of mechanisms is the author's own certified result rather than an independently established fact, and should be read in that register.

## One knob, and a claim that it cannot be removed

The framework's most distinctive technical contribution is a claim about parameter counting. Reduced to essentials, the construction carries a single dimensionless free parameter, κ = 1/2, which sets the overall normalization — *a₀* is exactly half the gravitational free-fall acceleration at the dark-energy density. Zimmerman's third 2026 paper is devoted to arguing that this κ is *unforceable* — that it cannot be fixed by ghost-freedom, unitarity, holographic bounds, or a Standard-Model degree-of-freedom count — and simultaneously that it is *pure geometry*: the single-degree-of-freedom limit of the Cohen–Kaplan–Nelson holographic bound, and an instance of the identity 3/8π = (Schwarzschild ½)/(ball 4π/3).

The rhetorical contrast is with ΛCDM's six parameters plus an as-yet-undetected particle sector. One geometric knob, the argument goes, against six numbers and a missing particle.

Here precision matters, and Zimmerman is careful about it: κ = 1/2 is a *posit*, not a derivation — in his own words, "a distinct, single, honest posit." The framework does **not** derive the *value* of *a₀*. It derives the *form* of the *a₀*–Λ relation and pins the normalization to a geometric choice that it then argues is the only natural one. This is a one-parameter theory, not a zero-parameter one, and readers should resist any summary that says the acceleration scale has been "computed from first principles." It has not. What has been done is to tie *a₀* to ρ_DE through a relation whose shape is heavily constrained and whose single coefficient is argued — not proven — to be geometrically inevitable. The value of Λ itself remains an input, taken from observation like any other.

## What is shared, and what is distinctive

It is essential to separate the framework's empirical fits from its novel content, because they are not the same set.

The relations de Sitter–MOND reproduces on real data — the radial-acceleration relation (RAR) linking observed to baryonic acceleration, charted in detail by McGaugh, Lelli and Schombert (2016) on the SPARC galaxy sample, and the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation (BTFR) linking rotation speed to baryonic mass — are **shared with the entire MOND family**. Any theory that reproduces Milgrom's low-acceleration limit reproduces these. They are evidence for the MOND phenomenology in general; they are not evidence for this framework specifically. A reader should treat a tight RAR fit here as table stakes, not as a discriminating success. The precise value of *a₀* that best fits SPARC rotation curves is itself convention-dependent — it shifts with the assumed stellar mass-to-light ratio, the interpolating function, and the scatter metric — so the closeness of 9.36 × 10⁻¹¹ to fitted values is reassuring but not, on its own, diagnostic of the framework's particular coefficient.

The genuinely framework-distinctive content is twofold. The first and sharpest is a prediction about cosmic history: if *a₀* is fixed by dark energy, then *a₀* must *track the dark-energy density through time*, *a₀*(*z*) ∝ √ρ_DE(*z*). Zimmerman is candid that the √ρ_DE scaling itself is not new — it appears in Limbach, Psaltis and Özel (2008) — and that the framework's contribution is to promote it from a possibility to a *forced* relation, with the data now sharp enough to test it. In a universe with a true cosmological constant, ρ_DE is constant and *a₀* never changes — indistinguishable from ordinary MOND. But under the evolving dark energy that DESI's second data release has reported (a *w₀wₐ* equation of state with *w₀* ≈ −0.75, *wₐ* ≈ −0.86), ρ_DE is not constant, and the framework predicts a specific, non-monotonic history: a roughly 6% rise to a phantom-divide crossing near redshift 0.41, followed by a decline to about 0.74 of today's value by *z* ≈ 3.

The cleanest observational handle is the *sign* of the high-redshift baryonic Tully–Fisher offset. If *a₀* was smaller in the early universe, distant galaxies should rotate slightly *slow* for their baryonic mass — of order 7% at *z* ≈ 3 — and crucially in the *opposite* direction from a rival "rising-*a₀*" reading that ties the scale to the total density *cH*(*z*), with the two branches diverging by a factor of roughly 7.5 at *z* = 3. Standard cosmology predicts no evolution at all. This is a clean three-way fork, and it is testable: DESI DR3 on a timescale of 2026–27, and high-redshift kinematics from the ELT, JWST and ALMA across roughly 2028–2032 and beyond.

The second distinctive prediction is more exotic. A preferred-frame, Lorentz-violating theory — which this is, by construction — is itself a background of the Standard-Model Extension (SME), the effective-field-theory catalogue of Lorentz violation. Zimmerman argues the framework therefore induces a computable, currently-bounded Lorentz-violation coefficient and a falsifiable theorem that any such violation is *Lorentz-breaking but CPT-preserving* — a structural signature open to laboratory and astrophysical tests rather than only to cosmology. It is worth being explicit that this is a consistency-and-interface argument: it induces a coefficient from the *a₀* already in hand and derives no Standard-Model parameter of its own. No new particle physics is produced here.

## The weak points, stated plainly

No honest account of this proposal can stop at its appeal, and Zimmerman's own papers do not. Three problems are serious.

**Lensing.** Relativistic completions of MOND have always struggled to bend light correctly without something that behaves like a dark-matter component, and de Sitter–MOND does not escape. Zimmerman's own work establishes a *no-go theorem*: under the framework's assumptions — general covariance, a luminal tensor mode, and ghost-freedom — a covariant, Solar-System-safe MOND lensing of the required kind is forbidden. The consequence is that gravitational lensing in the framework remains an *un-derived free function* — precisely the unfinished business that the leading relativistic-MOND theory, Skordis and Złośnik's AeST, also carries. This is not a peripheral gap. Lensing is where MOND-family theories have historically been most exposed, against both cluster mass maps and the cosmic microwave background, and the framework presently has no first-principles answer.

**Clusters.** Galaxy clusters retain a residual mass discrepancy that MOND does not fully cure, and de Sitter–MOND inherits it. In rich cluster cores, MOND typically still under-predicts the dynamical mass by a factor of a few, the gap that in ΛCDM is filled seamlessly by dark matter and that, in the Bullet Cluster and similar systems, appears spatially offset from the baryons in a way modified-dynamics theories find hard to reproduce. Zimmerman does not claim to have closed this; the most that can be said is that the deficit is shared with the wider MOND program rather than unique to his version, and that the framework offers no special escape from it.

**The DESI hostage.** The framework's distinctive *a₀*(*z*) prediction is non-diagnostic *today*, and it is hostage to a contested result. If the DESI evolving-dark-energy signal reverts to a plain cosmological constant — and some prior-sensitivity reanalyses suggest the evidence for evolution is fragile, with the significance dropping toward ∼1.3σ under certain prior choices, against the 2.8–4.2σ quoted for the headline DR2 result — then ρ_DE is constant, *a₀*(*z*) is flat, and the framework's novel cosmological content collapses back into ordinary, time-independent MOND. The sharpest thing the theory predicts about cosmic history is the thing most at risk of evaporating, and a reader should weight it accordingly.

To these one must add the structural limits, which the framework states against itself. It is, emphatically, *not* a theory of everything. It is a theory of gravity and the dark sector, and nothing else. It does not touch the Standard Model: the proton-to-electron mass ratio reduces to a free Yukawa coupling and the strong coupling and is not derivable; the Koide lepton relation is not derived; no particle masses and no gauge group emerge. Even the number of fermion generations is, in the framework, a fitted quantity rather than a prediction. The Standard Model is walled off — exactly as it is for every other current theory of gravity, but the point bears stating against any temptation to read the framework as more unifying than it is.

## How the community is likely to read it

It would misrepresent the state of the field to suggest this proposal is poised to overturn the consensus. It is not. Mainstream cosmology overwhelmingly favours particle dark matter within ΛCDM, and for concrete reasons. That model fits the acoustic peaks of the cosmic microwave background and their polarization to sub-percent precision; it reproduces the baryon-acoustic-oscillation scale across redshift in galaxy surveys; it predicts the primordial light-element abundances from big-bang nucleosynthesis with a single baryon density; and it accounts for cluster lensing and the growth of large-scale structure within one parameter set. No MOND-family theory has matched that breadth, and the few that come close to the cosmological data do so by reintroducing a dark component in another guise. Critics of the MOND program point, reliably and correctly, to clusters and to lensing as the places where modified-dynamics theories break down — and de Sitter–MOND, on its author's own showing, breaks down in exactly those places. This is one independent-researcher proposal, posted to an open repository, not yet through peer review and not established.

What distinguishes it from the large literature of dark-matter alternatives is mostly a matter of intellectual hygiene. The framework is unusually explicit about its own boundaries: it states what it derives (the form of the *a₀*–Λ relation), what it merely posits (the value, via κ = 1/2), what it shares with all of MOND (the galaxy-scale fits), what predates it (the √ρ_DE scaling), and what is uniquely its own and falsifiable (the *a₀*(*z*) history and the SME signature). It carries a self-imposed no-go theorem on its weakest flank rather than papering over it. And it makes a genuine, near-term, sign-level prediction that the next few years of high-redshift kinematics and DESI data can confirm or kill.

That is the better version of the story, and the more credible one. Not "researcher solves dark matter" — he has not, and does not claim to — but a single, economical idea that takes a fifty-year-old coincidence seriously, converts it into a falsifiable claim about cosmic history, and stakes itself on tests arriving this decade. If the DESI evolution signal firms up and high-redshift galaxies are found rotating slow in the predicted direction and by the predicted amount, the coincidence will look causal and the framework will demand a serious second look. If the signal reverts to a cosmological constant, or distant galaxies show no offset, the distinctive content dissolves and what remains is MOND with a tidy origin story for one number — and MOND's old, unhealed wounds at clusters and in lensing.

Either way, the proposal has the cardinal virtue the field should reward: it has told us, in advance and in specific numbers, what would prove it wrong.
