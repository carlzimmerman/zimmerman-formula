# The Galaxies' Borrowed Acceleration

### An independent researcher proposes that the mysterious scale governing galaxy rotation is set by dark energy itself. The idea is brave, economical, and falsifiable — and a decisive decade of measurements lies ahead.

There is a number that has haunted astrophysics for four decades, and almost nobody outside the field has heard of it.

It is an acceleration: roughly one ten-billionth of a meter per second, per second. Call it a₀. It is the acceleration a feather would feel falling under a gravity ten billion times gentler than Earth's — too faint to register on any instrument you have ever touched, the merest suggestion of a tug. And yet this whisper of a number appears to govern the architecture of every spiral galaxy in the sky.

Here is how it shows up. The stars in the outskirts of a galaxy orbit far too fast. By the seventeenth-century logic of Newton and Kepler — the same orbital mechanics that lands a probe on a comet — those stars, feeling only the gravity of the visible matter inside their orbits, should be flung outward like children losing their grip on a merry-go-round. They are not. They hold. For half a century the dominant explanation has been that galaxies are sheathed in vast halos of invisible matter — dark matter — whose extra gravity supplies the missing grip. It is the reigning paradigm, woven into the standard model of cosmology, and it works spectacularly well on the largest scales: the cosmic web, the afterglow of the Big Bang, the slow gravitational sculpting of structure across billions of years.

But down at the scale of a single galaxy, something strange surfaces. The rotation anomaly does not switch on at a particular distance, or a particular mass, or a particular brightness. It switches on at a particular *acceleration*. Wherever the gravitational pull on a star drops below a₀ — that same whisper-faint value, in galaxy after galaxy, dwarf and giant alike — the rotation curve goes anomalous. Above a₀, ordinary physics. Below it, the discrepancy blooms. The Israeli physicist Mordehai Milgrom noticed this in 1983 and built a phenomenology around it called Modified Newtonian Dynamics, or MOND, which posits that gravity — or inertia — simply behaves differently below a₀. MOND has spent forty years as the field's most productive heresy: scorned for its failures on galaxy clusters and gravitational lensing, yet uncannily, almost insultingly, accurate at predicting how individual galaxies spin.

And lurking inside a₀ is a coincidence that has tantalized physicists since the beginning. Take that tiny acceleration and ask what else in the universe carries the same magnitude. The answer is the cosmos as a whole. Multiply the speed of light by the Hubble expansion rate, or — more pointedly — compute the acceleration associated with dark energy, the mysterious pressure driving the universe's expansion to accelerate, and you land on the same ballpark figure. The number that rules the insides of galaxies is, to within a factor of a few, the number that rules the fate of the entire universe.

In the standard picture, this is an accident. A curiosity. Two unrelated regimes of physics that happen to rhyme.

Carl Zimmerman does not think it is an accident.

Zimmerman is not a professor. He runs a small software company, Briar Creek Tech, and builds custom applications for clients — the kind of work that has nothing to do with cosmology. He is, by the unforgiving taxonomy of academic physics, an outsider, an independent researcher, the category that arrives in editors' inboxes wreathed in justified suspicion. Over the past year he has posted three papers to the open repository Zenodo, none peer-reviewed, laying out a framework he calls de Sitter–MOND. The central claim is audacious in the way that good physics is audacious: not that it explains everything, but that it makes one specific, previously accidental coincidence into a law.

The claim is that a₀ is *made of* dark energy. Not approximately. Structurally.

To feel the shape of the idea, set aside the equations and consider what dark energy does to empty space. In a universe like ours — one with a cosmological constant, a baseline tension woven into the vacuum — space is not truly cold and quiet even when you remove every particle from it. There is a horizon, a maximum distance beyond which the accelerating expansion carries everything forever out of reach, and that horizon, like the event horizon of a black hole, has a temperature.

This is one of the strangest results in twentieth-century physics, and it is worth sitting with. In the 1970s, Stephen Hawking showed that a black hole's horizon glows faintly with thermal radiation; around the same time William Unruh showed that mere *acceleration* can conjure warmth from the vacuum — an accelerating observer registers a temperature where an inertial one finds only emptiness. The horizon of an accelerating universe combines both ideas. It, too, has a temperature: a de Sitter–Unruh temperature, named for the geometry of cosmic expansion and for Unruh's effect. It is the faintest imaginable warmth, far too cold to feel or measure directly. But it is not zero. Empty space, in a universe with dark energy, has an irreducible thermal floor. It cannot get colder than that, and the depth of that floor is set by the very same dark energy that is pushing the cosmos apart.

Now drop a star into the quiet outskirts of a galaxy, where it drifts in near–free fall, barely pushed. Zimmerman's mechanism — building on a 1999 proposal of Milgrom's and on a 1997 calculation by Stanley Deser and Janna Levin — says that the star *feels that thermal floor as resistance*. To accelerate it even slightly, you have to push against not just its mass but against the warmth of the vacuum it is embedded in. The push meets more inertia than Newton would have predicted. And extra resistance to being moved looks, from the outside, exactly like extra gravity holding the star in its orbit.

This is the conceptual hinge, and it is worth lingering on, because it is what separates Zimmerman's proposal from a generic restatement of MOND. He is not adding a force. He is modifying *inertia* — the stubbornness of matter, its reluctance to change its motion. The distinction has teeth. A modification of inertia tied to a thermal floor naturally switches *off* where accelerations are high, because there the vacuum's warmth is swamped by the ordinary heat of vigorous motion. In the solar system, where the planets feel the Sun's robust pull, the de Sitter–Unruh warmth is utterly negligible, and the correction vanishes — not by fine-tuning, but by construction. This is why the framework survives the most exacting test gravity has ever passed: the tracking of the Cassini spacecraft around Saturn, which confirmed Newtonian gravity to staggering precision. The framework's deviation there is some thirty times smaller than Cassini could have seen. An *ungated* modification — one that altered gravity as a force everywhere, the way a relativistic-MOND theory must — would have failed that test by orders of magnitude. The fact that a₀ hides itself in the solar system is not an excuse the theory makes. It is a prediction it keeps, and as we will see, it is the sharpest blade in the whole construction.

What gives the proposal its peculiar gravity — its seriousness — is not the mechanism alone but a particular kind of accounting that Zimmerman performs on it, with an honesty rare in this corner of physics.

The coincidence at the heart of it all is real, and it is the foundation: a₀ and the cosmological constant share a single number. Build a theory on that, and the question is how much of the rest you can force and how much you must simply assume. The form of the relation — that a₀ should go as the speed of light squared times the square root of the cosmological constant — turns out to be heavily over-determined. Zimmerman, working through the algebra with symbolic computation and an auditor's paranoia, finds that several structurally independent lines of reasoning all converge on the same form. An earlier version of his own work claimed seven such routes; a careful recount, which he reports against his own interest, collapsed three of them into one and left four. The correction is the kind of move that makes the rest more believable, not less — a researcher marking down his own scorecard in public. Even the strange half-integer power of pi buried in the coefficient — an odd, telltale √π that no simpler thermal argument produces — falls out of the gravitational bookkeeping, the factor of 8π from Einstein's equations married to the factor of 3 from the equations governing cosmic expansion.

When the dust settles, the entire unforced content of the theory — everything not fixed by geometry and known physics — reduces to a single number of order one. Zimmerman calls it kappa, and it equals one-half. It is, in his framing, pure geometry: a₀ comes out to exactly half the gravitational free-fall acceleration you would compute at the density of dark energy. One knob.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not mean, because the temptation to oversell is exactly what would sink the idea. Zimmerman does *not* derive the value of a₀. The factor of one-half is a posit — a clean, geometric, well-motivated posit, but a posit. The theory is one-parameter, not zero-parameter. What it offers is an economy. The standard model of cosmology runs on six parameters and an as-yet-undetected particle. This framework, on its own terms, runs on one number and no particle at all. Whether that economy reflects something true about nature, or merely a clever repackaging, is precisely the question the universe has to answer.

It does not take long, talking to physicists about this kind of proposal, to hear the objections, and they deserve their full weight.

The first is that the parts of the data Zimmerman's framework gets right, it shares with all of MOND. The radial acceleration relation — that tight, almost suspiciously clean correlation between how fast a galaxy's stars orbit and how much visible matter it contains — is a triumph, but it is MOND's triumph, reproduced by the entire family of modified-dynamics theories. The same is true of the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation, the iron link between a galaxy's mass and the fourth power of its rotation speed. None of this is unique to the de Sitter–MOND idea. A working cosmologist, presented with a successful galaxy fit, will rightly shrug: MOND has been doing exactly that for forty years.

And it still cannot account for galaxy clusters — the great agglomerations where hundreds or thousands of galaxies swarm in a common gravitational well, the largest bound objects in the universe. Here MOND's luck runs out. Measure the speeds of the galaxies, or the temperature of the hot X-ray gas pooled between them, and you can weigh the cluster. The visible matter falls short — and crucially, MOND's boost, calibrated on individual galaxies, does not close the gap. A stubborn residue of missing mass remains, perhaps a factor of two, exactly where dark matter does its most convincing work. Zimmerman's framework inherits this problem; its modified inertia softens the cluster discrepancy but does not dissolve it. He concedes the point. Clusters are where the dark-matter picture looks most like simple truth and where every MOND-family theory, his included, has the most explaining to do.

The second objection is sharper, and Zimmerman names it himself, which is to his credit. Gravitational lensing — the bending of light by mass — is where modified-dynamics theories have historically bled out. To predict lensing, you need a fully relativistic version of the theory, one that meshes with Einstein's general relativity. And here Zimmerman reports, in his own work, what amounts to a no-go theorem: under reasonable assumptions, you cannot build a version of this MOND that is simultaneously consistent with relativity, safe in the solar system, *and* able to predict lensing from first principles. The lensing, in his framework, remains an un-derived free function — a piece bolted on rather than grown. This is not a small caveat. It is the same unfinished business that hobbles AeST, the leading relativistic MOND theory, to which Zimmerman's construction turns out to be a close cousin: the two share the same deep structure on the simplest circular orbits but diverge elsewhere, a sibling rather than a completion. He does not paper over the gap. He marks it in red.

The third objection is the largest: this is not a theory of everything, and Zimmerman is unusually insistent on saying so. It does not touch the Standard Model of particle physics. It does not explain the masses of the electron or the proton, does not derive why there are three generations of matter, does not produce the gauge groups that organize the forces. "Not a theory of everything yet," he writes, "as frustrating as it may be." The Standard Model sits walled off — exactly as it does, to be fair, for every other theory of gravity currently on the table, including the textbook one. But the wall is there, and he draws it explicitly. This is a theory of gravity and the dark sector. Full stop.

Set against the towering empirical success of the standard cosmological model — the cosmic microwave background fit to exquisite precision, the large-scale structure of the universe reproduced in supercomputer simulations seeded with cold dark matter — a one-person proposal posted to a preprint server is, by any honest measure, a long shot. Most cosmologists hold, with good reason, that the particle dark matter picture remains overwhelmingly the best bet, and that MOND-family theories carry well-known liabilities at exactly the scales where dark matter shines. None of that is in dispute here. The proper frame is not *revolution*. It is *a provocative, economical, and genuinely falsifiable idea, facing a skeptical mainstream and a decisive decade.*

Because — and this is what makes it a story rather than a curiosity — it is falsifiable. And the test is coming.

Here is the prediction that separates de Sitter–MOND from ordinary MOND, the one piece of the framework that is distinctively its own.

If a₀ is made of dark energy, then a₀ cannot be a constant. As the density of dark energy changes over cosmic history, a₀ must change with it — tracking the square root of the dark-energy density across time. In the plain-vanilla picture, where dark energy is a fixed cosmological constant, this changes nothing: a constant density gives a constant a₀, and the framework quietly collapses back into ordinary MOND, its distinctive content dissolved. But dark energy may not be constant. The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, DESI, has in its recent data releases reported tentative evidence — contested, prior-sensitive, but persistent — that dark energy is *evolving*, that its pressure has shifted over billions of years.

Feed DESI's measured evolution into Zimmerman's formula and something remarkable happens. The acceleration scale does not simply rise or fall. It traces a non-monotonic arc: a roughly six percent rise to a gentle peak around redshift 0.4 — landing, strikingly, right at the cosmic moment when DESI's dark energy crosses a special threshold, the so-called phantom divide — and then a decline, dropping to about three-quarters of its present value by redshift 3, when the universe was a couple of billion years old.

This is a sharp, specific, and lonely prediction. Standard cosmology says a₀ does not evolve at all — cold dark matter has no acceleration scale to vary, so its curve is flat. A rival "rising" reading of the same coincidence, one that ties a₀ to the Hubble rate rather than to the dark-energy density, would have a₀ climbing steeply to several times its value. Zimmerman's curve threads between them: at redshift 3 it sits at about 0.74 of today's value, while the rising rival has shot up past four and standard cosmology holds at one. The three readings fan apart by roughly sixfold — three theories, three fates, cleanly distinguishable.

And the cleanest way to catch it is not to measure the faint number directly but to watch its *sign* in the rotation of ancient galaxies. The trick lives in the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation, that tight link between a galaxy's visible mass and its rotation speed. The relation is anchored by a₀: shift the acceleration scale, and you shift where galaxies are predicted to sit on it. If a₀ was smaller in the early universe, then a distant galaxy of a given mass should rotate slightly slow — about seven percent slow at redshift 3 — falling just below the relation that local galaxies obey. This is exactly the kind of thing the next decade of astronomy is built to see. Point the James Webb Space Telescope, or the ALMA radio array, or the coming Extremely Large Telescope at a star-forming disk as it was when the cosmos was a fifth of its present age; measure the visible mass from the starlight and gas, and the rotation speed from the Doppler shift of light across the spinning disk; and ask, simply, which side of the local relation it lands on. Standard MOND predicts these ancient galaxies sit exactly on it. The framework predicts they fall below. That is a directional test — a *sign*, not a delicate amplitude — and a sign is far harder to fake with measurement error or astrophysical mess. It is reachable this decade: by DESI's next data release, around 2026 or 2027, and by that giant-telescope spectroscopy through roughly 2032 and beyond.

There is one more distinctive thread, more exotic and more speculative, and it cuts in a wholly different direction. Because a modified-inertia theory secretly singles out a preferred cosmic rest frame — there is, in effect, a "true" state of being at rest, defined by the average flow of the universe — it quietly violates the perfect democracy of reference frames that is the bedrock of Einstein's relativity. That places it, formally, inside a well-studied catalog of symmetry-breaking theories that physicists hunt with exquisitely sensitive tabletop experiments: atomic clocks, trapped ions, resonant cavities, all watching for a faint directional grain in the laws of physics. Run Zimmerman's numbers through that machinery and they predict a tiny, calculable signal — a whisper of preferred direction — currently sitting just beneath the tightest experimental bounds. Sharper still, the framework comes with a theorem about *which* symmetry it breaks: it can violate the equivalence of reference frames while leaving untouched the deeper symmetry between matter and antimatter, a specific and falsifiable signature that distinguishes it from many of its rivals. It is a second front, narrower than the cosmological one but entirely real — a place where a theory of galaxies could, surprisingly, be cornered on a laboratory bench.

The honesty of the proposal is, in the end, also its vulnerability. Zimmerman has built a theory that wears its own neck on the block. The evolving-dark-energy signal that animates his prediction is itself contested; recent reanalyses, varying the statistical assumptions, have seen the evidence soften toward something closer to a plain cosmological constant — by some accounts dropping toward marginal significance. If that is where the data lands — if dark energy turns out to be constant after all — then the distinctive prediction evaporates, and de Sitter–MOND degrades gracefully into the ordinary MOND it was trying to surpass, carrying all of MOND's unsolved problems, the clusters and the lensing, along with it. The framework is, in Zimmerman's own word, a *hostage* to a measurement it cannot influence.

That is an uncomfortable place for a theory to stand. It is also, by the strict logic of science, the most honorable one. A proposal that cannot be killed is not a theory; it is a mood. Zimmerman has written down the conditions under which his idea dies, named the instruments that will execute the verdict, and dated the executions to within a few years.

What remains is a strange and arresting picture. In it, the reason a star at the rim of a distant galaxy refuses to fly off into the dark is not a halo of invisible particles cradling it, but the faint warmth of the universe's own horizon — the thermal echo of dark energy, pressing back against the star's motion. The very thing pulling the cosmos apart would be the thing holding its galaxies together. The largest fact about the universe and one of the smallest would be, at last, the same fact.

It is a beautiful idea. Beautiful ideas in physics are usually wrong, and their authors are usually the last to admit it. What is unusual here is that the author has done the admitting in advance — has drawn the wall around what his theory does not reach, conceded the lensing gap and the cluster residue and the untouched Standard Model, and staked the whole construction on the sign of a number measured in the light of galaxies that existed when the universe was young.

The light is already on its way. We will not have to wait long to learn whether the coincidence was ever a coincidence at all.
