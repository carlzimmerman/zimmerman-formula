# The Number Hiding in the Dark

### A quiet acceleration scale shows up in every galaxy we measure — and one independent researcher thinks the accelerating universe put it there. The telescopes that will test the idea are already on the sky.

If you have ever pointed a telescope at a spiral galaxy and let your imagination ride the faint outer arms, you have been looking, without knowing it, at one of the deepest unsolved puzzles in physics. Those outer stars are moving too fast. By every rule we trust about gravity, a star drifting in the thin outskirts of a galaxy — where there is almost nothing left to hold it — should crawl along its orbit like Neptune compared to Mercury, slower and slower the farther out it goes. Instead, it races. Rotation curves go flat. The edges of galaxies spin as if something enormous and unseen were out there gripping them.

For fifty years the standard answer has been dark matter: vast invisible halos of some particle we have never caught in a detector. It is a good answer, and most cosmologists hold to it for excellent reasons. But there is a second clue in the same data — quieter, stranger, and harder to explain away — and an independent researcher named Carl P. Zimmerman has built a careful, falsifiable proposal around it. His idea, which he calls a de Sitter–MOND framework, says that the unseen behavior of galaxies and the accelerating expansion of the universe are not two mysteries. They are one.

### What you're actually seeing

Start with what the data show, because the data are genuinely beautiful.

When astronomers measure how fast stars and gas orbit in galaxy after galaxy, and plot the gravity the visible matter *should* produce against the gravity the orbits *actually* reveal, the points do not scatter into a cloud. They fall along a tight, clean line. This is the **radial-acceleration relation** — one of the most striking empirical laws in modern astronomy. Where the expected gravitational pull is strong, the relation is boringly Newtonian: predicted and observed match. But below a certain threshold of acceleration, the observed pull peels away from the prediction and runs high. Galaxies that look nothing alike — giant spirals, faint dwarfs, gas-rich disks — all bend at the *same* threshold.

That threshold has a value: roughly **1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared**, written a₀. It is an almost unimaginably gentle acceleration — a snail's pace of a push, a nudge so faint you would need the patience of cosmic time to notice it building. And it shows up everywhere, like a fingerprint pressed into the universe.

Here is the coincidence that has haunted physicists since the 1980s. If you take the cosmological constant — the dark energy that is pushing the universe apart, measured in an entirely different way, from supernovae and the cosmic microwave background — and ask what acceleration *it* sets, you get a number maddeningly close to a₀. Two completely unrelated regimes, the dusty outskirts of galaxies and the empty stretch of the expanding cosmos, seem to share a single scale.

Most researchers have filed this under "probably a coincidence." Zimmerman's framework takes the opposite stance: it is causal.

### Dark energy as a temperature floor

The mechanism is where the idea earns its keep, and it is more elegant than it has any right to be.

In a universe with dark energy, empty space is never truly cold. A cosmological constant gives the vacuum an irreducible temperature floor — a faint, unavoidable warmth predicted by the same family of physics that gives black holes a temperature. It is fantastically small, but it is not zero. Building on the de Sitter–Unruh temperature derived by Stanley Deser and Janna Levin in 1997, and on Mordehai Milgrom's modified-inertia program, Zimmerman's proposal says that this floor is what sets a₀.

The move is subtle. The framework does not add a new force or sprinkle invisible matter into halos. It modifies **inertia** — an object's resistance to being pushed. A star coasting in near-free-fall in a galaxy's faint outskirts, barely accelerating at all, feels that vacuum temperature floor as a slight extra reluctance to change its motion. Extra inertia, seen from the outside, looks exactly like extra gravity. The star holds its fast orbit not because unseen mass is pulling harder, but because the star itself has become, in a sense, harder to slow down.

The pieces fit together to give

> a₀ = (c/2)·√(G·ρ_DE)

— the acceleration scale of galaxies written directly in terms of the density of dark energy. Plug in the measured numbers and you get about **9.4 × 10⁻¹¹ m/s²**, comfortably in the neighborhood of the observed a₀.

Because it is a modification of inertia rather than an added force, it has a built-in safety feature that particle dark matter lacks: it switches **off** where accelerations are high. In the Solar System, where planets feel the Sun's firm grip, the effect vanishes — which is exactly what the Cassini spacecraft's exquisite tracking of Saturn demands. The theory is Solar-System-safe by construction, not by fine-tuning.

### One knob

What makes Zimmerman's version worth a serious look — beyond the appealing story — is its economy. The standard model of cosmology runs on six adjustable parameters plus an as-yet-undetected dark-matter particle. This framework, Zimmerman has argued in a published one-parameter theorem, runs on a single geometric knob, a factor he writes as κ = ½. He makes the case that this value cannot be tuned away — that it is fixed by the geometry itself, falling out of the same kind of bookkeeping that gives a sphere its volume and a black hole its horizon.

That economy is the genuine appeal. One geometric posit, no new particle, and the galaxy threshold and the cosmic acceleration come from the same root.

It is important to be honest about what that one knob means, and Zimmerman is. The framework does **not** derive the *value* of a₀ from nothing — κ = ½ is a posit, so this is a one-parameter theory, not a zero-parameter miracle. And it is emphatically not a theory of everything. It says nothing about why the proton outweighs the electron, nothing about particle masses or the forces of the Standard Model. "It's not a theory of everything yet," Zimmerman says of his own work, "as frustrating as it may be." It is a theory of gravity and the dark sector, full stop.

### The prediction you can watch for

Here is what lifts this above a fifty-year-old coincidence and turns it into science you can put a date on.

If a₀ really is set by dark energy, then a₀ should **change** as the dark-energy density changes across cosmic history. Galaxies at high redshift — galaxies as they were billions of years ago — should show a *different* threshold than galaxies today. Plain dark-matter cosmology has no fundamental acceleration scale built in, so it makes no specific prediction for how a₀ should change; it can be made to fit almost any trend after the fact, but it forecasts none. Zimmerman's framework, by contrast, forecasts a precise one. The two pictures part ways in the distant universe — and the distant universe is precisely what our biggest telescopes are now built to see.

The specifics depend on how dark energy itself behaves, and here the framework leans on a genuinely contested measurement. Recent results from the **DESI** survey hint that dark energy may not be a constant but may be slowly evolving. That hint is real but disputed: depending on how the data are combined with other surveys, its statistical strength has been quoted as high as roughly three to four sigma in the headline analyses, but prior-sensitivity reanalyses have pushed it down toward about 1.3 sigma — barely a flicker. The entire distinctive part of Zimmerman's prediction rests on that one disputed input. If the evolving-dark-energy signal holds, the framework predicts a distinctive, non-monotonic history for a₀: a roughly **6 percent bump** near redshift 0.4, then a decline to about three-quarters of today's value by redshift 3 — the era when the universe was only about two billion years old, its first galaxies freshly assembled. The cleanest place to catch it is in the **baryonic Tully-Fisher relation**, the tight link between how fast a galaxy spins and how much ordinary matter it contains. The framework predicts that those very distant galaxies should rotate about **7 percent slow** — a small, specific, measurable offset, with a definite *sign*.

Which telescopes, and when? **DESI**, already gathering millions of spectra, sharpens the dark-energy measurement that the whole prediction hangs on, with its next data release expected around 2026–27. The high-redshift rotation tests fall to the giants: the **James Webb Space Telescope** and **ALMA** are already measuring how early galaxies spin, and can begin probing the sign of that Tully-Fisher offset over roughly 2028–2030. The coming **Extremely Large Telescope** will push resolved kinematics deep into the early universe around 2030 and beyond. This is not a prediction for some far-off century. It is a prediction for this decade, testable with instruments either on the sky now or under construction.

### What honesty requires

A good idea deserves to be reported with its weaknesses in full view, and this one has real ones.

The galaxy relations the framework fits so well — the radial-acceleration relation, the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation — are **shared** with the entire MOND family of modified-gravity theories. They are not unique fingerprints of Zimmerman's version. The genuinely distinctive content is narrower: the evolving a₀(z) signature, and a tiny predicted Lorentz-violation effect in the Solar System (the framework, being tied to a preferred cosmic frame, is itself a testable "Standard Model Extension" with its own falsifiable theorem).

Gravitational lensing — the bending of starlight by mass — is a genuine sore spot. A no-go theorem established in this very work shows that a fully covariant, Solar-System-safe MOND lensing cannot be built in the obvious way, leaving the framework's lensing as an un-derived free function. That is the same unfinished business carried by the leading relativistic MOND theory, AeST; it is not a solved problem here.

Galaxy clusters are a second sore spot, and a long-standing one for this whole class of ideas. Even with the inertia modification switched on, clusters retain a residual mass discrepancy — extra gravity the visible matter and the framework together still cannot fully account for. Zimmerman's framework, like MOND generally, narrows that gap but does not close it.

And the headline prediction is, in the end, a **hostage to DESI**. If the hint of evolving dark energy melts back into a plain cosmological constant — and as noted, some reanalyses already push it to the edge of significance — then the distinctive a₀(z) signature dissolves, and the framework reverts to ordinary MOND, with all of MOND's known troubles. This is one independent researcher's published proposal, posted openly to the Zenodo repository in 2026 in three papers — the framework's spine, the a₀(z) prediction, and the one-parameter theorem — and not yet through the gauntlet of peer review. The overwhelming weight of mainstream cosmology still favors particle dark matter and the standard model, for reasons built on decades of converging evidence.

But here is why it is worth your attention the next time you frame a spiral in your eyepiece. The history of astronomy is full of "coincidences" that turned out to be doorways — Kepler's tidy orbital ratios, the matching of inertial and gravitational mass that became Einstein's elevator. Zimmerman has taken one of the oldest unexplained coincidences in the field, given it a clean mechanism, pared it down to a single geometric knob, and — most importantly — staked it on a specific number that the next decade of telescopes will measure. He may well be wrong. But he has done the honorable thing: he has told us exactly how to find out. So when you watch those outer stars race around a galaxy faster than they have any right to, remember that the universe may have written the same gentle number into the dark between the galaxies. We are about to learn whether that is a coincidence — or a clue.
