# What if dark matter is really dark energy in disguise?

### *A lone researcher says the universe's two biggest mysteries are secretly the same number. The galaxies seem to agree. The hard part is everything else.*

There is a number that haunts the outskirts of galaxies. Out where the stars thin and the orbits slow, gravity stops behaving the way Isaac Newton — and Albert Einstein after him — said it should. Stars circle far too fast for the visible matter to hold them. For 90 years, the mainstream answer has been dark matter: vast invisible halos of some undiscovered particle, outweighing ordinary matter five to one, scaffolding every galaxy in the cosmos.

But the anomaly has a curious tell. It switches on only below a particular acceleration — roughly 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ metres per second squared, a ten-billionth of the tug you feel standing on Earth. Above that threshold, in the bustling inner galaxy and throughout our Solar System, gravity is impeccably Newtonian. Below it, the rules bend. This scale has a name, a₀, and it has been an unexplained brute fact of nature since the Israeli physicist Mordehai Milgrom first flagged it in 1983 with his theory MOND — Modified Newtonian Dynamics.

Here is the coincidence that won't leave people alone. Take the cosmological constant, the dark energy pushing the universe to accelerate apart, and ask what acceleration *it* sets. You get a number within a whisker of a₀. Two mysteries at opposite ends of the cosmos — the slowest dance in the galactic suburbs and the fastest expansion of all spacetime — appear to be tuned to the same dial. Physicists have noticed this for half a century. Most have filed it under coincidence and moved on.

Carl Zimmerman would like to un-file it.

## One number, two mysteries

Zimmerman is not a professor. He is an independent researcher who runs a software company, Briar Creek Tech, in North Carolina, and works on physics in the cracks of his week. Over 2026 he has posted three papers to the open repository Zenodo laying out what he calls a de Sitter–MOND framework. The pitch is bracingly simple: the galaxy scale isn't *near* the dark-energy scale by accident. It is *set* by it.

In his bookkeeping, a₀ = (c/2)√(G ρ_DE) — the speed of light, the gravitational constant, and the density of dark energy, bolted together with a single geometric factor. Plug in the measured dark energy and out comes 9.36 × 10⁻¹¹ metres per second squared, comfortably inside the range galaxies actually prefer. No tuning. One equation linking the cosmos's fast end to its slow one.

What's appealing here, even to sceptics, is that the *form* of that equation isn't plucked from the air. The shape a₀ ~ c²√Λ — the cosmological constant under a square root — falls out of four independent lines of reasoning, and the numerical kernel, including an awkward-looking half-power of π, is pinned by the same factors of 8π and 3 that appear in Einstein's equations and the standard expanding-universe model. It is the kind of over-determination that makes physicists sit up, because it is hard to fake.

It is worth being clear about what this buys and what it doesn't. At today's epoch, the framework simply *is* MOND — it inherits MOND's whole track record on galaxies, the good and the bad. What it adds is a claimed reason *why* a₀ has the value it does, and a way for that value to drift. Hold that thought; it matters later.

## Inertia, not a fifth force

The mechanism is where the framework gets genuinely interesting — and where it parts company with most dark-matter alternatives.

Zimmerman does not add a new force or a new particle. He leans on a subtle prediction of quantum theory in an expanding universe: empty space in a cosmos with a cosmological constant has an irreducible temperature floor, a faint thermal hum known as the de Sitter–Unruh effect. The proposal, building on a 1999 idea of Milgrom's and earlier work by Stanley Deser and Janna Levin, is that a star drifting almost weightlessly in a galaxy's outskirts *feels* that hum as a slight extra resistance to being pushed. Its inertia goes up. From the outside, more sluggish stars look exactly like stars held by extra gravity — like dark matter.

The elegance is that this is a modification of *inertia*, not of gravity. It only matters when accelerations sink near that cosmic temperature floor. Crank the acceleration up — as in the Solar System, where the planets are briskly whipped around the Sun — and the effect vanishes automatically. That is why the framework sails through the exquisitely precise tracking of NASA's Cassini probe at Saturn, which has ruled out many a modified-gravity scheme. Here, Solar-System safety isn't bolted on. It is baked in.

And there is a real claim to economy. Zimmerman argues — and devotes a whole paper to arguing — that the framework has exactly *one* free knob, a geometric one-half he calls κ, and that this knob can't be wiggled without breaking deep consistency requirements like the absence of runaway "ghost" energies. One dial, he points out, against the standard cosmological model's six parameters plus a dark-matter particle that, after 40 years of searching, no experiment has caught.

## The test it can't dodge

A pretty story is not a theory. What lifts this above numerology is that it sticks its neck out.

If a₀ really is set by dark energy, then as the density of dark energy changes over cosmic history, a₀ must change too — it should track √ρ_DE back through time. Standard MOND treats a₀ as a fixed constant of nature. Standard cosmology, with a true unchanging cosmological constant, predicts no variation either. Zimmerman's framework predicts a specific wiggle.

And here the timing is almost too good. The DESI survey, mapping millions of galaxies, has reported hints that dark energy is *not* perfectly constant — that it may be gently evolving. Feed DESI's numbers into Zimmerman's formula and you get a distinctive, non-flat curve: a roughly 6 per cent bump in a₀ near redshift 0.4, then a decline to about three-quarters of today's value by redshift 3. The cleanest place to catch it is in the rotation of very distant galaxies: a₀ riding lower in the deep past should make galaxies at redshift 3 spin about 7 per cent slow, in a measurable direction, against a flat-MOND baseline.

That is a falsifiable, near-term prediction. DESI's next data release, expected around 2026–27, will sharpen the dark-energy trend; the giant telescopes coming online — the Extremely Large Telescope, alongside JWST and ALMA — should reach the high-redshift galaxy kinematics needed to look for the offset over the following years. The framework has, in effect, named the experiment that could kill it.

## Now the cold water

All of which makes a stirring story — and a responsible one has to pour the cold water itself, because the gaps are real and Zimmerman, to his credit, names them first.

Start with the obvious: **this is not a theory of everything.** "Not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be," is how Zimmerman puts it. It is a theory of gravity and the dark sector, and nothing else. It says nothing about the Standard Model of particle physics — no particle masses, no explanation of why the proton outweighs the electron by 1836, no gauge groups. That whole edifice sits walled off, exactly as it does for every rival theory.

And it does **not derive the value of a₀.** That geometric one-half, κ, is posited, not proven from first principles. So this is a one-parameter theory, not the zero-parameter holy grail. It is leaner than ΛCDM, not magic.

The sharpest technical wound is **gravitational lensing** — how the framework bends light around galaxies and clusters. Zimmerman's own work proves a no-go theorem: you cannot build a fully consistent, Solar-System-safe version of this lensing within the obvious relativistic rules. So lensing is left as an un-derived free function, patched in by hand — the very same unfinished business that hangs over AeST, the leading relativistic cousin of MOND. Critics will, fairly, push hard here.

There is also a humbling point about what the framework can claim as its own. The headline galaxy successes — the tight relation between visible matter and rotation speed, the famous link between a galaxy's brightness and its spin — are **shared with the entire MOND family.** They are not unique evidence for *this* idea. What is distinctive is the evolving a₀ and a faint, Solar-System-scale signature of a preferred cosmic frame. Everything else, a MOND theorist could also claim.

Which leads to the deepest "if" of all. The whole distinctive case **hangs on dark energy genuinely evolving** — and that is contested. The DESI hint is suggestive, not settled; reanalyses with different statistical priors have seen it soften, in some treatments down towards a marginal 1.3-sigma flicker. If dark energy turns out to be a plain, unchanging cosmological constant after all, Zimmerman's distinctive prediction evaporates and the framework collapses back into ordinary MOND — with MOND's well-known baggage, including a stubborn residual mass discrepancy in galaxy clusters that neither MOND nor this framework fully cures.

And it is not as though the evolving-a₀ idea is winning on the evidence so far. There is already one shot on goal, and it has missed in the framework's direction. The first direct attempt to measure a₀'s drift over cosmic time — a 2026 study of distant galaxies with the MUSE instrument — saw the scale apparently *rising* towards the past, at around redshift 1, not falling as Zimmerman's declining branch requires. The signal is contested and softens to roughly two-sigma once instrumental systematics and a ΛCDM look-alike reading are folded in, so it is no kill. But it is not the framework's friend either, and Zimmerman himself flags it as the place his idea is most exposed.

Finally, it must be said plainly: this is one independent researcher's proposal, not peer-reviewed, not established, swimming against a strong tide. The overwhelming majority of cosmologists still back particle dark matter and the standard ΛCDM model, which fits a vast sweep of data — the cosmic microwave background, the cosmic web, the abundance of the light elements — that MOND-family theories have long struggled to match across every scale at once. Most working cosmologists would call the a₀–dark-energy coincidence just that: a coincidence, until shown otherwise.

## Why it's worth watching anyway

Here is what keeps the idea from being dismissable. It is brave, it is economical, and — unlike a great deal of theoretical cosmology — it tells you exactly how to prove it wrong. It takes a 50-year-old coincidence that the mainstream has been content to shrug at, and insists on a causal explanation with a price tag: a specific, measurable bending of a₀ through cosmic time, arriving for judgement this very decade.

If the distant galaxies come back rotating dead on the flat-MOND prediction, with no sign of the evolving offset, the distinctive idea is finished. If DESI's evolving dark energy melts back into a constant, likewise. But if the high-redshift galaxies spin a touch slow, in just the way the dark-energy density says they should — then a software engineer from North Carolina will have a serious claim on the thread tying the universe's slow dance to its runaway expansion.

The smart money is still on dark matter. It usually is. But this is a rare beast in modern physics: a clean, falsifiable idea that has named the experiment that will hang it or crown it, and the experiment is already being built. Whichever way it breaks, we get an answer. And in a field where so many beautiful theories float forever out of reach of any telescope, that alone is worth the watching.
