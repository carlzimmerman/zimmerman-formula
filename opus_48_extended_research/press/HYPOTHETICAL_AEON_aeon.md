# The acceleration at the edge of the galaxy

### A lone researcher proposes that the mysterious scale governing how galaxies spin is made of dark energy. The idea is brave, economical, and — for once — falsifiable. It is also a lesson in how beauty can mislead.

There is a number that has haunted the study of galaxies for half a century, and almost nobody outside a narrow guild of physicists has heard of it. It is an acceleration: roughly one ten-billionth of a metre per second, per second. To feel how faint that is, consider that the acceleration you experience standing still on the Earth's surface, held down by its gravity, is about ten metres per second per second. The number that haunts the galaxies is one hundred billion times gentler than that — a nudge so slight that a falling object subject to it alone would take centuries to build up the speed of a strolling pedestrian. Call it a-naught, written *a₀*. It is, as far as anyone can tell, woven into the structure of the cosmos.

What makes *a₀* strange is what happens at it. Inside galaxies, where gravitational accelerations are stronger than this threshold — in the bright crowded discs where the stars are dense — gravity behaves exactly as Newton and Einstein say it should. But cross below *a₀*, out in the thin dark margins where a star drifts almost weightless on the galaxy's edge, and something changes. The stars there orbit far too fast. They should, by the visible mass alone, be flung outward or, more precisely, should fall back into slower orbits the way the outer planets crawl compared to Mercury. Instead they hold formation, circling at speeds that imply five times more gravity than the luminous matter can provide. The discrepancy switches on, cleanly and universally, right around that one impossibly small acceleration.

For most of the last forty years, the field's overwhelming answer to this has been *dark matter*: a vast, invisible halo of some unknown particle cradling every galaxy, supplying the missing pull. It is a good answer in the sense that it works extraordinarily well across the sweep of cosmic history — the microwave afterglow of the Big Bang, the growth of the great filaments of galaxies, the abundances of the light elements. The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM, built on this invisible particle plus a cosmological constant (the Lambda) driving the universe's accelerating expansion, is one of the most successful theories in science. It is also, after four decades and a parade of billion-dollar detectors tunnelled deep underground, still waiting for anyone to actually catch the particle. The gravitating *stuff* is real, betrayed by its tug on light and on the early universe. The *particle* remains a promissory note.

And there is that other oddity, the one the dark-matter picture has never quite been able to explain away. The acceleration scale *a₀* — a number drawn from the dynamics of individual galaxies, a parochial, sub-galactic quantity — turns out to be suspiciously close to a number drawn from the entire cosmos. Multiply the speed of light by the rate at which the universe is expanding and you land within shouting distance of *a₀*. More tellingly, the acceleration you'd compute from dark energy itself — the mysterious pressure inflating the void, the largest and emptiest fact about reality — comes out, to a factor of order one, equal to the gentle nudge that governs the edges of galaxies. Two numbers from opposite ends of the universe, the smallest gravitational whisper and the grandest cosmic push, landing on the same value. The field has long acknowledged this coincidence. It has, for the most part, simply labelled it and moved on.

Carl Zimmerman does not want to move on. He thinks the coincidence is the whole story.

---

Zimmerman is not a professional cosmologist. He runs a small software company in the Carolinas — builds customer-management systems for clients, the unglamorous machinery of working software. He is, in the genre's slightly worn phrase, an independent researcher, which in physics is a category freighted with suspicion, and not without reason: the gap between the cranks and the genuine outsiders is wide, and the cranks are louder. But the proposal he has spent the last stretch of years sharpening — he calls it de Sitter–MOND — and published openly to the Zenodo repository in 2026 across three papers, deserves to be taken on its own terms — partly because it is unusually honest about its own limits, and partly because it makes the one move that most theories in this overgrown corner of physics refuse to make. It puts its neck on the block.

The idea, stripped to a sentence, is this: the galactic acceleration scale *a₀* is *made of* dark energy. Not coincidentally near it — caused by it. Write it as an equation and it is almost insolently compact: *a₀* equals the speed of light squared, times the square root of the cosmological constant divided by thirty-two pi. Feed in the measured value of Lambda and out comes a number, 9.4 hundredths of a billionth of a metre per second squared, sitting right where the galaxies say it should. The coincidence of fifty years becomes, in this telling, a causal identity. The scale at which galaxies go anomalous is simply the acceleration that the dark energy of the universe imposes on everything in it.

How could the emptiness of intergalactic space reach into a galaxy and change how a star resists being pushed? The mechanism Zimmerman leans on is genuinely beautiful, and it is not his invention — it descends from work by Mordehai Milgrom, the physicist who first noticed *a₀* in 1983, and from a thermodynamic insight by Stanley Deser and Janna Levin. Here it is. A universe with a cosmological constant is never truly cold. The accelerating expansion wraps every observer in a horizon, a far cosmic boundary beyond which things recede faster than light, and that horizon — like the edge of a black hole — radiates the faintest possible warmth. Empty space has a temperature floor it can never drop below. Now take a star drifting in the dark outskirts of a galaxy, almost in free fall, accelerating so gently it barely feels itself move. Such an object, the argument goes, feels that irreducible cosmic warmth as a kind of friction — a resistance to being accelerated, which is to say, *extra inertia*. From outside, extra resistance to a push is indistinguishable from extra gravity holding the star in. There is no missing matter. There is only the universe's thermal floor, making the most weightless objects slightly harder to move.

The elegance of this is that it explains, almost for free, why the effect is invisible in the Solar System. Modified-gravity theories have long had a problem: if you change how gravity works to fix the galaxies, you tend to wreck the exquisitely measured orbits of the planets, where Newton reigns to many decimal places. But this is a *modified inertia* theory, not a modified force. The extra resistance only matters when accelerations are tiny, below *a₀*. In the Solar System, where the Sun's pull is enormous, the effect switches off by construction. The Cassini spacecraft, which tracked Saturn's gravity to spectacular precision, sees nothing — and the theory predicts it should see nothing. The thing turns itself off exactly where we have looked hardest.

---

What should an honest reader do with this? The temptation, with an idea this clean, is to fall for it — and the danger of falling for beauty is precisely the meditation this proposal forces.

Physics has a long and not entirely happy romance with elegance. The conviction that the true laws of nature must be beautiful — symmetric, economical, mathematically inevitable — has guided some of the deepest discoveries (Einstein reasoned his way to general relativity as much by demands of consistency and symmetry as by data) and has also led whole communities into barren decades chasing supersymmetric particles and elegant unifications that the colliders stubbornly declined to produce. Beauty is a guide and a seducer in the same gesture. A coincidence as clean as *a₀* equalling the dark-energy acceleration *feels* like it must mean something. But the history of science is also a graveyard of meaningful-feeling coincidences that meant nothing — numerical near-misses that dissolved under better measurement, patterns the human pattern-hungry mind imposed on noise. The honest question is never "is this beautiful?" It is "what would prove it wrong, and has anyone tried?"

This is where Zimmerman's proposal earns its hearing, because it answers that question, and answers it against itself.

Begin with what is genuinely strong, credited at full weight. The coincidence is real; it is not manufactured. And the *form* of the equation — that *a₀* should go as the speed of light squared times the square root of Lambda — turns out to be over-determined, which is to say several independent lines of reasoning force that same shape. The particular geometric factor in it, that thirty-two pi with its strange half-integer power of pi, is not pulled from a hat: the eight-pi traces directly to the eight-pi that sits in Einstein's field equations, the three to the factor of three in the Friedmann equation governing cosmic expansion. The skeleton of the formula is fixed by the architecture of gravity itself. This is the opposite of numerology, where one fishes for any combination of constants that lands near a target. Here the structure is dictated, and only one knob is left free.

That single knob is the most striking part. Zimmerman's recent work — the third of his papers — is a sustained argument that the one free parameter in the theory, a geometric factor he calls kappa, fixed at one-half, cannot be derived from deeper principles and cannot be eliminated either. He shows it survives every attempt to pin it down by the usual theorists' tools — the requirements of a healthy quantum theory, the holographic bounds on information, a counting of the Standard Model's degrees of freedom. It is, he argues, pure geometry: the one-half is the Schwarzschild factor that appears in every black hole, divided by the four-thirds-pi volume of a ball; the whole thing is the single-degree-of-freedom limit of a known bound on how much energy a region of space can hold. One number, posited, not derived — and proven to be the *only* number you must posit. Set that against the standard model of cosmology, which carries six free parameters and, beyond them, an entire undetected particle. On the ledger of parsimony, one knob against six-plus-a-ghost is not nothing. Parsimony is a real argument, and here it points one way.

And then, decisively, the proposal makes a prediction that can kill it.

---

If *a₀* is literally made of dark energy, then it cannot be a constant. Dark energy's density, for all we know, may change over cosmic time — and if it does, *a₀* must change with it, tracking the square root of the dark-energy density through the history of the universe. This is the part the standard model structurally cannot match, and not because it is shy: in the dark-matter picture there simply *is* no acceleration scale to evolve. Cold dark matter has no *a₀* hiding in it; the tightness of the galactic relations is, in that picture, an emergent regularity to be explained by the messy physics of gas and feedback, not a fundamental constant with a value and a history. Zimmerman's theory welds *a₀* to a quantity — the dark-energy density across time — that an entirely separate enterprise of cosmology is busy measuring. He has staked a curve on a number he does not control.

And here the story acquires real tension, because that number has lately begun to move. The DESI survey, mapping millions of galaxies to chart the expansion, reported in 2024 and 2025 a tentative signal that dark energy may not be constant after all — that it is, mildly, evolving. Fed through Zimmerman's fixed relation, that evolving dark energy produces a specific, peculiar prediction: *a₀* should not simply decline into the past but trace a non-monotonic track — rising about six per cent to a gentle peak around redshift 0.4, at precisely the moment the equation of state crosses a particular threshold, then falling away to about three-quarters of its present value by redshift three. The sharpest way to test it is almost embarrassingly concrete: distant galaxies, seen as they were when the universe was a fifth its current age, should rotate about seven per cent too slowly relative to their nearby cousins, sitting just *below* the relation that local galaxies obey. Ordinary, fixed-scale modified gravity predicts they sit *on* it. A rival reading predicts they sit well above. Three theories, three falsifiable fates, separated at high redshift by a factor of more than seven. The instruments to settle it — DESI's next data release, then the high-redshift galaxy kinematics that the Extremely Large Telescope, JWST, and ALMA will deliver across the late 2020s and into the 2030s — are coming. This decade will decide it.

There is something bracing about an idea so exposed. Most of what fills the modified-gravity literature can absorb almost any result; feedback and free functions are infinitely accommodating. Zimmerman's curve cannot hide. It will be right or it will be wrong, and the data that judges it is already being gathered by people who have never heard his name and have no stake in his being right. That is what a scientific claim is supposed to look like.

---

But honesty cuts both ways, and the proposal's own papers are unusually scrupulous about the wounds. A reflective reader has to carry these at full weight, because they are where the seduction must be resisted.

It is not a theory of everything, and Zimmerman says so plainly — "not a theory of everything yet, as frustrating as it may be." It is a theory of gravity and the dark sector, full stop. It does not touch the Standard Model of particle physics: it cannot tell you why the proton outweighs the electron by the factor it does, cannot derive the masses of the quarks or the pattern of the leptons, says nothing about why there are three families or why the forces have the strengths they have. That entire edifice sits walled off — exactly, it must be said, as it sits walled off for every other current theory of gravity, including Einstein's. But walled off it is.

Nor does the theory derive the *value* of *a₀*. That one free knob, kappa equals one-half, is a geometric posit, not a theorem. The theory is one-parameter, not zero-parameter, and the difference matters: it explains the *scale* of the coincidence, names the geometric factor, but does not hand you the precise number from nothing. To claim otherwise would be to lie.

There is a genuine weak point in gravitational lensing — the bending of light by mass, one of the most powerful tools in all of cosmology. A theory of modified gravity must say how light bends, and Zimmerman's own work establishes, by a no-go theorem he proves rather than evades, that there is no way to build a fully consistent, Solar-System-safe version of this lensing within the covariant rules the theory wants to obey. The framework's lensing remains an un-derived free function — the same unfinished business that the leading relativistic cousin of MOND, the theory called AeST, has long carried. This is conceded, not hidden, and it is real.

The galaxy relations the theory fits so cleanly — the tight correspondence between visible matter and observed motion, the relation between a galaxy's mass and its rotation speed — are not unique to it. They are shared with the entire MOND family of theories, a phenomenology that several proposals reproduce. The genuinely *distinctive* content of Zimmerman's framework is narrow: the evolution of *a₀* through cosmic time, and a faint Lorentz-violating signature it predicts in the Solar System. Being a preferred-frame theory, it quietly becomes a testable background for the broader search for violations of relativity — itself a small, elegant consequence, and itself bounded by current experiments. Strip away the *a₀(z)* prediction and what remains is, observationally, ordinary modified gravity.

Which leads to the hostage. The distinctive prediction lives or dies on whether dark energy really is evolving — and that DESI signal is contested. Reanalyses that vary the statistical priors have found the evidence softening, in some treatments dropping toward marginal significance. If the signal reverts, if dark energy turns out to be a plain, unchanging cosmological constant after all, then *a₀* doesn't evolve, the distinctive curve flattens into a line, and the whole framework dissolves into garden-variety MOND — with MOND's well-known, unsolved problems, chief among them the galaxy clusters, where this theory, like its relatives, leaves a residual mass discrepancy it does not fully cure. Zimmerman's most interesting claim is held hostage by a measurement he cannot influence, and the captor's resolve is uncertain.

And the largest caveat of all, the one no amount of internal rigour can dissolve: this is one independent researcher's proposal. It is published openly but it is not peer-reviewed, not established, not vetted by the slow adversarial machinery that turns a clever idea into accepted physics. The overwhelming weight of professional cosmology favours particle dark matter and the standard model, for reasons that are good and earned — that model's track record across the CMB and the growth of structure is something no modified-gravity theory has matched. The mainstream skepticism is not mere conservatism; it is the accumulated judgment of a field that has watched a great many beautiful alternatives fail to survive contact with the full sweep of the data. The reasonable prior is that this one, too, falls short somewhere. Most do.

---

So what is at stake, and why dwell on the proposal of a software entrepreneur from the Carolinas at all?

What is at stake is one of the genuine forks in our picture of reality. Either roughly a quarter of the universe is an exotic particle we have not yet found and may yet find — and the galaxies spin fast because they are cradled in halos of it — or our theory of gravity and inertia is incomplete at the faintest accelerations, and there is no missing matter, only missing physics. These are not small alternatives. They imply different universes. The dark-matter universe is mostly invisible stuff obeying familiar laws; the modified-inertia universe is familiar stuff obeying unfamiliar laws, with the emptiness of space reaching into every galaxy to set the terms. We do not yet know which we live in. Anyone who tells you the question is closed is telling you about the sociology of the field, not about the evidence.

And what it would *mean*, if Zimmerman's specific bet came in — if the distant galaxies really do rotate that crucial few per cent slow, on the curve his equation drew before the data arrived — is something worth sitting with. It would mean that the gentle threshold governing the edge of every galaxy is not an independent fact about gravity but a downstream consequence of the largest fact about the cosmos, the dark energy tearing space apart. It would mean that the dynamics of a single star at a galaxy's rim and the fate of the entire universe are the same physics, read at different scales. The smallest acceleration we can measure and the grandest would be one number, because they are one cause. That is the kind of unification physicists dream about, and it is exactly the kind of dream one should be most suspicious of, precisely because it is so satisfying.

This is the epistemics worth holding onto. A one-parameter idea that names the experiments that will kill it is not automatically *true* — beauty and economy and even falsifiability are not truth — but it is *better science* than a six-parameter paradigm leaning on an undetected particle and declaring itself complete by default. Not because parsimony guarantees correctness; it does not. But because the one-parameter idea is *exposed*, and exposure is the whole game. The standard model of cosmology measures the things it ought to explain — it measures Lambda without deriving it, measures the dark matter without catching it — and there is no shame in that; measuring is how science begins. But a theory that stakes a definite curve on a number it cannot control, and invites the world to falsify it within a decade, has done the braver and, in the end, the more useful thing. It has made itself mortal.

The danger, the one this whole meditation circles, is that the very features which make Zimmerman's proposal compelling — the elegance of the mechanism, the inevitability of the form, the clean closure of a fifty-year coincidence — are also exactly the features that have, again and again, led good physicists to believe wrong things. The cosmos is under no obligation to be economical. The coincidence may yet be a coincidence; the form may be a mirage that several mechanisms produce because the dimensional analysis leaves them little choice; the *a₀(z)* signal may flatten and take the distinctive content with it. The correct posture toward a beautiful, falsifiable, unconfirmed idea is not belief and not dismissal. It is *attention* — the willingness to let the data, now actually being gathered, decide a question that elegance alone cannot.

Carl Zimmerman has done the rare and honourable thing: he has built an idea brave enough to be wrong, and he has told you where to point the telescope. The galaxies at the edge of the visible universe are spinning, right now, at speeds that either lie on his curve or do not. Within this decade we will know. Until then, the only intellectually honest response is the one the best physics has always demanded — to admire the structure, distrust the admiration, and wait for the sky to answer.
